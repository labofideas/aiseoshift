Someone considering Botox for the first time in 2026 does not call a clinic. They ask ChatGPT whether Botox or Dysport lasts longer, use Google AI Overviews to summarize "how much do lip fillers cost," and ask Perplexity which med spa in their area has the most experienced injectors. The clinics named in those answers get the consultation. The clinics that are not named never enter the decision.

Aesthetic medicine sits close to the YMYL line. These are medical procedures performed on the body, under physician supervision, with real risk. AI engines apply healthcare-grade scrutiny to who is allowed to be recommended. The med spas winning in 2026 have verifiable medical oversight, treatment pages written with clinical accuracy, and structured data that makes the practice machine-readable.

This is the playbook.

## Quick answer

The four things that drive med spa SEO in 2026: a treatment-specific page for every procedure you offer, visible medical-director and injector credentials, Google Business Profile per location with real before-and-after results and active reviews, and aesthetic [schema markup](/seo/schema-markup/) that makes the clinic and its providers machine-readable. Anonymous "expert team" pages with no named medical oversight do not get cited in aesthetic AI answers.

## Why med spa SEO changed in 2026

Three shifts:

1. **AI Overviews answer treatment and cost queries.** "Does Botox hurt," "how long do lip fillers last," "Botox vs Dysport," "how much is laser hair removal." The clinics cited as sources benefit. Everyone else is invisible.
2. **Aesthetic medicine inherited YMYL filters.** These are medical procedures, and AI engines treat them that way. A clinic with no visible medical director or licensed-injector information is filtered out of recommendations.
3. **Buyers compare obsessively before they book.** A consultation is a high-consideration decision involving cost, safety, and trust. The clinics with the deepest, most honest treatment and pricing content win the comparison.

For broader context, see our [AI SEO Shift pillar](/ai-seo-shift/) and the [E-E-A-T guide](/seo/eeat/).

## Pillar 1: Treatment-specific pages

Patients search by treatment, not by clinic. Each procedure you offer needs its own dedicated page, written with clinical accuracy and reviewed by the medical provider who performs it.

Treatments that should each have their own page:

- Botox and other neuromodulators (Dysport, Xeomin, Daxxify)
- Dermal fillers (lips, cheeks, under-eye, jawline)
- Laser hair removal
- Laser skin resurfacing and IPL
- Microneedling and microneedling with PRP
- Chemical peels
- Body contouring (CoolSculpting, Emsculpt)
- Medical-grade facials and skincare
- Hormone and weight-management programs where offered

Each page should cover what the treatment is, who it suits, what to expect during and after, realistic results and how long they last, risks and contraindications, honest cost ranges, and FAQs. Bylined to the licensed provider who administers it.

## Pillar 2: Medical oversight and injector credentials

This is the trust pillar, and it is where aesthetic medicine differs from a salon. Every clinical page needs:

- Named medical director with credentials and license
- Named injectors and providers (MD, DO, NP, PA, RN) with their licensure and training
- `Person` schema with `hasCredential` for each provider
- "Reviewed by [Provider], [credential]" notation on treatment pages

A med spa is a medical setting. AI engines, and increasingly patients, check that there is real physician oversight and that the person holding the syringe is licensed to do so. Anonymous content fails this test.

## Pillar 3: Google Business Profile and real results

Each location needs its own GBP, fully built and actively maintained.

The non-negotiables:

- Primary category (Medical Spa) plus relevant secondary categories (Skin Care Clinic, Laser Hair Removal Service)
- Services list with the treatments you offer
- Real, consented before-and-after photos, not stock images
- 30+ photos including providers, treatment rooms, and the space
- Weekly posts (new treatments, provider spotlights, seasonal offers)
- Q&A seeded with common patient questions
- Active review velocity system

Before-and-after authenticity matters. Stock or borrowed images get detected and erode trust. Real, consented results from your own patients are both a conversion driver and a signal of a legitimate practice.

## Pillar 4: Aesthetic medical schema

Structured data makes the practice and its providers machine-readable. Every med spa needs:

- `MedicalBusiness` or `HealthAndBeautyBusiness` schema on the homepage
- `Person` schema with `hasCredential` for the medical director and each provider
- `Service` or `MedicalProcedure` schema on treatment pages
- `FAQPage` schema on treatment and FAQ pages
- `Review` schema where reviews are displayed

See the broader [schema markup guide](/seo/schema-markup/) for full implementation.

## What AI engines verify before recommending a med spa

The four pillars make you eligible. Eligibility is not a recommendation. Before an engine names a clinic for "best med spa near me" or cites it on "is Botox safe," it runs a quiet trust check, and much of it happens on signals outside your own site.

**Medical oversight, verifiably.** Is there a named medical director, and is the person performing injections licensed to do so in your state? State medical and nursing boards are public. A clinic that names its medical director and injectors, with credentials that check out, reads as legitimate. One that hides behind "our expert team" reads as a risk in a near-YMYL category.

**Name, address, and phone consistency.** The clinic name, suite, phone, and hours, identical across your site, GBP, and the aesthetic directories (RealSelf, Yelp, Google). Conflicts lower confidence everywhere.

**Before-and-after and review authenticity.** Engines and patients both discount stock photos and generic five-star reviews. Real consented results and specific, recent reviews that name treatments and providers read as a real, active practice.

**Recency and accuracy.** Aesthetic products and protocols change. A treatment page reviewed in the last six months, citing current products and realistic outcomes, beats a stale page that overpromises. The "reviewed by [provider] on [date]" line is a freshness signal engines read.

This is the same diligence a careful patient does before letting someone inject their face. Win by making the evidence easy to find.

## Pricing, packages, and the cost question

Aesthetic treatments are paid out of pocket, and "how much does [treatment] cost" is among the highest-intent queries in the category. Most clinics answer it nowhere, so the AI answer gets built from a directory or a competitor.

Address cost directly, in the words patients use:

- Per-unit or per-syringe pricing for injectables, and how many units a typical area needs
- Honest ranges for laser packages, body contouring, and series-based treatments
- Membership or loyalty pricing, and any financing (Cherry, CareCredit)
- What the consultation costs and whether it applies to treatment
- What drives cost up or down for each treatment

A page that says "Botox is $12 to $14 per unit, a typical forehead treatment runs 20 to 30 units, and we offer membership pricing and CareCredit financing" answers the question that decides whether someone books. AI engines lift these concrete numbers and named programs into answers. "Contact us for pricing" gives them nothing.

## Common mistakes that keep clinics invisible

- **"Our expert team" instead of named, licensed providers.** The most common failure in a near-YMYL category. No named medical oversight, no citation.
- **Stock before-and-after photos.** Detected, discounted, and a trust killer. Use real, consented results only.
- **Thin treatment pages.** "We offer Botox" loses to a page covering units, results, longevity, risks, and cost.
- **Ignoring the cost question.** The highest-intent aesthetic query, answered nowhere.
- **Stale content with no review date.** Aesthetic protocols move; an old page reads as abandoned.
- **Thin or missing schema.** No `MedicalBusiness`, no `hasCredential`. Readable by humans, invisible to the systems doing the citing.
- **Treating it like a salon.** Aesthetic medicine carries medical risk and medical scrutiny. The trust signals are clinical, not cosmetic.

Fix the named-provider oversight and treatment-page depth first. They gate trust in this category.

## The query types that book consultations

Stop chasing "med spa near me." The queries that produce consultations are deeper:

- **Treatment + location:** "lip filler [city]," "laser hair removal [city]"
- **Question + treatment:** "how long does Botox last," "does microneedling help acne scars"
- **Comparison:** "Botox vs Dysport," "CoolSculpting vs Emsculpt"
- **Cost:** "how much are under-eye fillers," "laser hair removal full body cost"
- **Concern + solution:** "best treatment for jowls," "how to fix smile lines without surgery"

Lower volume individually, higher intent, lower competition. These are the queries AI engines synthesize answers for.

## What this looks like operationally

A 90-day plan:

- **Month 1:** Provider bio pages with credentials and schema. GBP rebuild with real before-and-after. First 5 treatment pages.
- **Month 2:** 5 more treatment pages. Pricing and membership pages. Schema deployment sitewide.
- **Month 3:** Comparison and concern-based content. Review velocity stable. Performance review.

Twelve months in: 15+ treatment pages, full provider credential infrastructure, 100+ recent reviews per location, and consultations from treatment and cost queries competitors do not target.

## FAQs

### How long does med spa SEO take to show results?

Most clinics see measurable improvement in three to six months. Local pack and GBP work shows fastest, often within 60 to 90 days. Treatment pages can rank within four to eight weeks for less-competitive long-tail queries.

### Is aesthetic content treated as YMYL?

Close to it. These are medical procedures with real risk, and AI engines apply healthcare-grade scrutiny to who can be recommended. Treat every treatment page as if a quality rater is checking for licensed provider authorship and medical oversight.

### Should the website show injector and medical-director names?

Yes. Named, licensed providers with verifiable credentials are the core trust signal in aesthetic medicine. Anonymous "our team" content fails extraction and reads as a risk to patients comparing clinics.

### Should treatment pages show prices?

Yes, as honest ranges and per-unit figures. "Botox $12 to $14 per unit, membership pricing available" answers the question that decides whether someone books, and AI engines extract these concrete numbers when competitors stay vague.

### Can AI write med spa content?

For first drafts, with significant rewrite and full review by the licensed provider. AI-drafted aesthetic content without provider review fails extraction and carries medical-accuracy risk.
