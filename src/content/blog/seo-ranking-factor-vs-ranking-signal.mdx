Someone tells you "backlinks are a ranking factor." Someone else tells you "the number of referring domains is a ranking signal." Both statements are true, and they are not saying the same thing.

Most SEO writing uses "factor" and "signal" interchangeably. Google does not.

The mix-up would not matter if it stayed academic. It does not stay academic. It shows up in content briefs that say "improve E-E-A-T" with no next step. It shows up in vendor pitches that promise to fix your "ranking factors" without naming a single thing they will actually change. Get the distinction straight and the vague advice gets a lot harder to hide behind.

## Ranking factor: the category

A ranking factor is a group name. "Content quality." "Backlinks." "Page speed." "Mobile usability." When someone lists "Google's top 10 ranking factors," they are naming buckets, not measurements.

The word is useful shorthand. It is also where most vague SEO advice lives, because a bucket is not a task. Nobody can "improve authority" directly. They can build a specific link from a specific relevant domain. Authority is the factor. The link is closer to the signal.

"Factor" is a practitioner word, not Google's official vocabulary. Google rarely uses it in its own documentation, and that gap is itself a clue about how loosely the term gets applied across the industry.

## Ranking signal: the measurement

A signal is the thing an algorithm can actually read. It has a value. It can be scored, thresholded, or compared.

Under the factor "page experience" sit real signals: Largest Contentful Paint, Cumulative Layout Shift, whether the page is served over HTTPS, whether it passes a mobile-friendly check. Google has confirmed Core Web Vitals function as ranking signals, and they are exactly this: specific, numeric, measurable. We covered how HTTPS specifically functions as one of these trust-adjacent signals in [HTTPS and security signals for AI SEO](/blog/https-security-signals-ai-seo/).

Under the factor "authority" sit signals like referring-domain count, the topical relevance of linking pages, and anchor text distribution. Under "relevance" sit signals like whether the query terms and their synonyms appear in the title, the heading structure, and the body copy in proportion to how a human would actually write about the topic.

| Factor (the category) | Example signals underneath it |
|---|---|
| Page experience | LCP, CLS, HTTPS status, mobile usability pass/fail |
| Authority | Referring-domain count, link relevance, anchor diversity |
| Relevance | Title-query match, heading structure, topical coverage |
| Freshness | Last-modified date, update frequency, query recency need |
| Content quality | Depth versus competing pages, factual specificity, structure |

Notice the pattern. Every factor decomposes into two or more signals, and the signals are the part you can actually act on.

## Ranking system: Google's own word

Here is where most explanations of this topic stop short, because they miss a third term Google uses deliberately: ranking system.

A ranking system is a named algorithm or model, the thing that actually reads signals and produces a judgment. The Helpful Content System is a ranking system. So is the core ranking system, and the reviews system. Google documents these directly in its [Guide to Google Search Ranking Systems](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ranking-systems-guide).

Google has been explicit that this is a real, distinct category, not a synonym for "signal." Its own guidance states plainly that ranking systems are different from ranking signals, because systems typically make use of signals. Google even pruned items from its public ranking-systems list after concluding some of them were actually just signals mislabeled as systems, not standalone algorithms in their own right.

That correction matters. Page Experience, for one, is officially a signal, not a system. It never had its own algorithm running underneath it the way the Helpful Content System does. It is an input that feeds other systems.

So the full chain runs: a signal is a measurement, a system reads a collection of signals and produces a score or judgment, and a factor is the informal bucket practitioners use to group related signals together when they talk about the result.

## Where this gets misused

Two patterns show up constantly, and both come from collapsing factor and signal into one word.

**Vendor claims that name a factor and stop.** "We optimize your E-E-A-T" sounds like an action. It is a label. E-E-A-T is not even a direct ranking factor in the strict sense, Google has said it informs the quality-rater guidelines that shape how the algorithm gets tuned, rather than being read as a single input itself. The actionable version names something specific underneath it: a verifiable author bio linked to real credentials, a citation to a primary source, a correction date on an updated claim.

**Content briefs that stay at factor level.** "Improve content quality" tells a writer nothing. "Add the specific weight, dimension, or price the competing pages leave out" tells them exactly what to do. The gap between those two sentences is the gap between a factor and a signal, and it is the difference between a brief that gets executed well and one that gets guessed at.

Site-wide judgment works the same way. When Google talks about evaluating a domain holistically, as it did in its comments on [site-level signals](/blog/site-level-signals-ai-seo/), it is describing signals aggregated across many pages, feeding into a system, producing an outcome practitioners then describe with a factor-level label like "site quality."

## Why the distinction is worth keeping straight

Naming the signal is what makes SEO work testable. You can measure whether LCP improved. You cannot measure whether "user experience" improved, not directly, because "user experience" is not a number anywhere in Google's systems. It is your summary of several numbers that are.

The practical habit is simple. Every time a brief, a pitch, or your own plan uses a factor word, ask what signal sits underneath it. If nothing specific comes to mind, that is usually the sign the work has not actually been scoped yet.

Factors are how we talk about rankings. Signals are what actually gets measured. Systems are what do the measuring.
