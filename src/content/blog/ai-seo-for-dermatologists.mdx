Dermatology practices have two distinct patient types searching for them, and AI engines treat them very differently.

The medical dermatology patient types a symptom: "red itchy rash won't go away," "what does melanoma look like," "dermatologist for psoriasis near me." These are YMYL queries — Google's strictest content category — and AI Overviews answer them with sourced, credentialed content. Practices without board-certified dermatologist bylines, clinical depth, and proper schema are invisible before the search results page even loads.

The cosmetic dermatology patient types a procedure: "best Botox injector in [city]," "laser resurfacing for acne scars," "filler near me." These are commercial queries with high purchase intent. AI engines respond differently — they synthesize reputation signals, review volume, and before/after evidence alongside credential signals. A practice winning both query types runs two parallel content strategies out of one building.

This is the playbook for both.

## Quick answer

The four things that drive dermatology SEO in 2026: a Google Business Profile built for both medical and cosmetic query types, dedicated pages for every procedure and condition the practice treats, a condition-specific review velocity system, and [schema markup](/seo/schema-markup/) that marks up the dermatologist, procedures, and medical conditions in machine-readable structured data. Practices running a single undifferentiated website for both medical and cosmetic services leave ranking potential on the table for both query types.

## Why dermatology SEO changed in 2026

Three shifts reshaped how patients find dermatologists:

1. **AI Overviews answer both health and beauty queries.** "What causes cystic acne," "how long does Botox last," "is psoriasis contagious" — AI Overviews synthesize these answers before any links appear. The dermatology practices cited as sources earn brand impressions at scale. Understanding [what AI visibility means](/blog/what-is-ai-visibility/) for your practice is the starting point.
2. **YMYL and commercial intent run in parallel.** Medical dermatology content — acne, eczema, psoriasis, skin cancer screening, Mohs surgery — faces the same strict YMYL scrutiny as any clinical health content. Cosmetic content — Botox, fillers, laser treatments — faces commercial query competition where reputation and visual evidence carry outsized weight. Both sides require different content strategies under one roof.
3. **Local pack results for derm queries are AI-influenced.** "Dermatologist near me," "cosmetic dermatologist [city]," and "Mohs surgeon [city]" now surface AI-summarized local recommendations. Practices with strong GBP signals, active review velocity, and structured data are the ones AI engines surface first.

The broader context for healthcare practices is covered in our [AI SEO playbook for medical practices](/blog/ai-seo-for-medical-practices-2026/).

## Pillar 1: Google Business Profile for dermatology

GBP is where local derm patients convert. The dermatology practice has a more complex GBP task than most healthcare specialties because it serves two distinct patient populations with different search behavior.

### Category strategy

Primary category should be **Dermatologist** for most practices. If the practice offers significant cosmetic volume alongside medical dermatology, secondary categories matter:

- **Skin Care Clinic** — captures cosmetic procedure searches
- **Medical Spa** — appropriate if the practice operates a med spa component
- **Cosmetic Surgeon** — only if a fellowship-trained cosmetic surgeon is on staff

Adding secondary categories without the corresponding clinical staff to support them invites credibility issues in AI extraction. Add only what the practice genuinely offers.

### Photo strategy for two patient types

Medical derm patients want to see the clinical environment — exam rooms, dermatoscopy equipment, Mohs surgery suite, phototherapy units. Cosmetic derm patients want before/after results, treatment rooms, and the injector's aesthetic.

A photo set for a full-service derm practice should include:

- Board-certified dermatologists and PAs in clinical settings
- Before/after galleries (covered below under E-E-A-T)
- Mohs surgery suite or specialized equipment
- Laser and energy-based device room
- Waiting area and office environment
- Staff in action during non-clinical moments

Weekly GBP posts should alternate between medical and cosmetic angles: a skin cancer awareness post one week, a laser resurfacing results post the next.

The mechanics of GBP healthcare optimization are covered in detail in our [GBP playbook for dental practices](/blog/google-business-profile-for-dentists/) — the framework transfers directly to dermatology.

## Pillar 2: Procedure and condition pages

This is the content layer where dermatology practices either dominate AI search or disappear entirely. The practice needs dedicated pages for every condition and procedure it treats — not a single "services" page listing everything.

### Medical dermatology condition pages

Each condition gets its own page, bylined to the treating board-certified dermatologist:

- **Acne** — including cystic acne, hormonal acne, acne scarring, and the range of treatments (topicals, oral medications, isotretinoin, laser, chemical peels)
- **Eczema (atopic dermatitis)** — triggers, flare management, biologic treatments
- **Psoriasis** — plaque, guttate, nail psoriasis; topicals, phototherapy, biologics
- **Rosacea** — subtypes, triggers, laser and topical management
- **Skin cancer screening** — what screening involves, ABCDE criteria, who should be screened and how often
- **Mohs surgery** — what it treats, what to expect, cure rates; the [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/treatment/mohs-surgery) is the authoritative external reference for Mohs indications
- **Suspicious moles and lesion removal**
- **Seborrheic dermatitis, contact dermatitis, urticaria**

Each page covers: what the condition is, who gets it, how it is diagnosed, treatment options the practice offers, what to expect at the appointment, and a FAQ section. The physician who treats the condition should be named as author.

### Cosmetic dermatology procedure pages

Each procedure gets its own page, bylined to the performing physician or PA:

- **Botox and neuromodulators** — forehead lines, crow's feet, glabellar lines, preventive Botox
- **Dermal fillers** — lip filler, cheek filler, under-eye treatment, jawline contouring, specific brands offered
- **Laser resurfacing** — ablative vs non-ablative, CO2 laser, Fraxel, indications and downtime
- **IPL and photofacial**
- **Microneedling and PRP**
- **Chemical peels** — superficial, medium, and deep peels; conditions treated
- **Body contouring** (if offered)
- **Sclerotherapy for spider veins**
- **Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) for hair loss**

Cosmetic procedure pages need one element medical condition pages do not: **social proof integrated into the content**. Before/after photo galleries embedded on the page, with clear patient consent documentation, function as ranking signals and conversion signals simultaneously.

## Pillar 3: Review strategy for dermatology

Reviews drive the local pack and AI recommendations for healthcare providers. Dermatology has a review complexity that dental and primary care practices do not: patients are often treating something they feel self-conscious about. The review ask needs to be sensitive to that.

### Timing the request

For medical dermatology: send the review request 24 to 48 hours post-appointment, once the patient has had time to process the visit. Same-day requests feel transactional when someone has just discussed a skin cancer diagnosis or a chronic condition management plan.

For cosmetic procedures: 48 to 72 hours post-treatment is ideal — long enough for initial swelling to reduce and for the patient to feel confident about their experience, short enough that enthusiasm is high.

### Condition-specific language

The review request should never reference the condition treated — that is a HIPAA violation. But the prompt can be written to elicit condition-specific language organically:

Rather than "We treated your acne today — please leave a review," use "We hope your appointment with Dr. [Name] went well. If you're happy with your experience at [Practice], we'd appreciate a Google review."

Patients who feel heard will naturally describe what they came in for. This produces review text that contains the keywords — "acne treatment," "Botox," "eczema," "skin cancer screening" — that AI engines extract as trust signals, without the practice prompting HIPAA-sensitive language.

### Platform diversification

Google is primary. For dermatology, **RealSelf** and **Healthgrades** are the secondary platforms that matter most. RealSelf carries specific weight for cosmetic procedure reputation because patients researching Botox or laser resurfacing frequently read RealSelf reviews during the research phase. Being well-represented there before they reach your GBP reinforces the eventual local search decision.

Local search ranking factors for healthcare practices — including review signals — are analyzed in research from [Moz's local search ranking factors studies](https://moz.com/local-search-ranking-factors), which consistently show review quantity and recency as top local pack signals.

## Pillar 4: Schema for dermatology practices

Structured data is the machine-readable layer that lets AI engines verify what the practice is, who treats patients, and what they treat.

### Core schema stack

**Practice level:**

```json
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Dermatologist",
  "name": "[Practice Name]",
  "medicalSpecialty": "Dermatology",
  "url": "[URL]",
  "address": { ... },
  "telephone": "[Phone]",
  "openingHours": [ ... ],
  "hasOfferCatalog": {
    "@type": "OfferCatalog",
    "name": "Dermatology Services"
  }
}
```

**Physician level** — one per dermatologist on staff:

```json
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Physician",
  "name": "Dr. [Name]",
  "medicalSpecialty": "Dermatology",
  "hasCredential": {
    "@type": "EducationalOccupationalCredential",
    "credentialCategory": "Board Certification",
    "recognizedBy": {
      "@type": "Organization",
      "name": "American Board of Dermatology"
    }
  }
}
```

**Procedure pages** — `MedicalProcedure` schema for cosmetic procedures, `MedicalCondition` for condition pages. The [schema markup guide](/seo/schema-markup/) covers implementation for both types.

**FAQPage schema** on every procedure and condition page. AI engines extract FAQ schema directly for AI Overview panels.

The detailed implementation for physician schema and medical procedure markup is covered in our [physician E-E-A-T and credentials infrastructure guide](/blog/physician-eeat-credentials-infrastructure/).

## E-E-A-T for dermatology: the signals that build AI trust

Dermatology has a specific E-E-A-T architecture because the practice operates at the intersection of YMYL medical content and high-value commercial content.

### Board certification and fellowship training

Every clinical page needs to signal the treating physician's qualifications. For dermatology, the key credentials are:

- **American Board of Dermatology** certification — the baseline credential that establishes board-certified status
- **Fellowship training** — Mohs surgery fellowship, cosmetic dermatology fellowship, dermatopathology fellowship
- **Academic affiliations** — hospital staff positions, university appointments
- **AAD membership** — the [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org) is the authority organization AI engines recognize for dermatology expertise

Each physician bio page should list these credentials explicitly, with the schema `hasCredential` markup matching the visible text. AI engines verify E-E-A-T claims by cross-referencing visible content with structured data.

### Before/after galleries as E-E-A-T evidence

Before/after galleries are the single most powerful E-E-A-T signal for cosmetic dermatology that most practices implement incorrectly. The gallery is not just a conversion tool — it is evidence of real-world experience and results.

To function as an E-E-A-T signal, galleries need:

- Individual cases attributed to the specific physician or PA who performed the procedure
- Written patient consent documentation referenced (not the details, just that consent exists)
- Procedure name and approximate timeframe labeled on each case
- Gallery embedded on the relevant procedure page, not siloed in a separate "gallery" section with no contextual content around it

Anonymous before/after grids with no attribution to a physician do not build E-E-A-T. They look like stock images to both patients and AI engines.

## FAQs

### How is dermatology SEO different from general medical practice SEO?

Dermatology practices run two parallel search strategies simultaneously — medical YMYL content for condition-based queries and commercial intent content for cosmetic procedure queries. General medical practice SEO focuses almost entirely on the YMYL side. Dermatology requires both, with separate content architecture, different review platforms, and different E-E-A-T signals for each side of the practice.

### Should a dermatology practice have one website or separate sites for medical and cosmetic?

One website with clearly separated content silos is the right structure for most practices. Separate websites split domain authority and create operational complexity. The exception: a practice operating a distinct med spa brand should build that brand with its own GBP and potentially its own domain, while maintaining cross-links to the parent practice for credential transfer.

### Do Botox and filler pages need physician bylines?

Yes. Cosmetic procedures performed by a physician require physician attribution — even though the query intent is commercial rather than informational. Procedures that can be delegated to PAs or nurse practitioners under physician supervision should still be attributed to the supervising physician, with the performer noted. This matters both for YMYL compliance and for state medical board regulations in many states.

### How many reviews does a dermatology practice need to rank in the local pack?

There is no universal threshold, but competitive dermatology markets in major metros typically require 150+ Google reviews at a 4.5+ rating to appear consistently in the three-pack. More importantly: review recency matters. A practice with 300 reviews but nothing in the last six months is outranked by a practice with 80 reviews and 15 in the last 90 days.

### What is the difference between Dermatologist schema and MedicalProcedure schema?

`Dermatologist` schema (a subtype of `Physician`) marks up the practice and its doctors as entities — who they are, their credentials, their location, their specialty. `MedicalProcedure` schema marks up individual procedure pages — what the procedure is, its type (surgical vs non-surgical), its body location, and its indications. Both are necessary. The practice schema establishes trust; the procedure schema enables AI extraction for individual procedure queries.

### How long does dermatology SEO take to show results?

GBP and local pack improvements are typically visible within 60 to 90 days of a complete rebuild. Condition and procedure pages begin ranking for long-tail queries within 6 to 12 weeks. Competitive head terms — "dermatologist [major city]," "Botox [major city]" — take 6 to 18 months depending on market density. The practices that start earliest compound the advantage fastest.
