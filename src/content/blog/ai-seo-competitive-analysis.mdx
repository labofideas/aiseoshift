Most businesses that discover they are not being cited by ChatGPT or Perplexity start by auditing their own site. That is a reasonable first move, but it answers only half the question. The other half — why your competitors are being cited instead of you — requires looking outward. Competitive analysis for AI SEO is different from traditional competitive analysis because the signals that determine citation are not always the same signals that determine organic ranking. A competitor with a weaker domain authority than yours might be cited far more frequently because they have structured content in a format AI engines prefer, better author credentials, or a schema implementation that makes their expertise legible to language models.

This guide walks through a structured process for identifying which competitors appear in AI-generated answers, understanding what signals produce their citations, and building an action plan that closes the most impactful gaps first. The framework is built around six measurable citation signals, a matrix approach for comparative scoring, and a prioritization method based on gap size and implementation effort.

## Quick answer: three-step AI competitive analysis

The core process runs in three steps. First, identify which competitors are being cited by testing the queries your category owns across ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews — this gives you a list of competitors that have solved the AI visibility problem you are trying to solve. Second, analyze what those cited competitors have in common: their content structure, schema implementation, author credentials, and off-site signals. Third, map the gap between what cited competitors have and what your site currently has, prioritize the gaps by citation impact, and execute the closure plan in order.

The entire process can be completed in a week of focused work. The output is a citation matrix that shows you exactly where you stand relative to the competitors winning your queries, and which specific changes would move you from absent to cited.

For the measurement framework that tracks citation rates before and after implementing changes, the [AI SEO KPIs and metrics guide](/blog/aeo-kpis-metrics-ai-visibility/) provides the full tracking methodology to use alongside this competitive analysis.

## Why traditional competitive analysis misses AI visibility

Traditional SEO competitive analysis focuses on keyword rankings, backlink profiles, domain authority, and on-page optimization. Tools like [Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com) and [Semrush](https://www.semrush.com) provide deep data on all of these dimensions. That data is still useful — domain authority and backlink quality are citation signals — but it is insufficient on its own for understanding AI citation performance.

The reason is that AI engines evaluate content along dimensions that traditional SEO tools do not measure. An AI engine deciding whether to cite a page for a factual query is not primarily asking "how many backlinks does this page have?" It is asking: does this page contain a clear, well-structured answer to the question? Does the author have demonstrable expertise in this topic? Is there schema markup that makes the entity and claims on this page machine-readable? Are there external corroborating signals — reviews, mentions, citations — that confirm the expertise claims?

A competitor might have a weaker backlink profile than you but still receive more AI citations because they have implemented FAQ schema on every how-to page, because their author bio pages include credentials that AI engines can parse, or because their content uses the definition-then-explanation structure that language models reliably extract and cite.

Traditional tools will not show you these gaps. You need to look directly at the pages that are being cited and reverse-engineer what they have done.

The [AI SEO audit checklist](/blog/ai-seo-audit-checklist/) provides the full framework for auditing your own site against these dimensions — use it in parallel with the competitive analysis to compare your scores directly against competitors'.

## Step 1: Identify which competitors are cited by AI engines

The starting point is manual query testing. This is not automatable in a meaningful way, because the goal is not just to record which domain appears — it is to understand the full citation context: what is the AI saying, what source does it cite, and how is the cited content framed.

**Build your query list first.** Start with the ten to fifteen queries most important to your business. These should be the informational and decision-stage queries where someone is actively seeking expertise in your category — "best [category] tools for [use case]," "how to [core problem in your domain]," "what should I look for in [product or service type]." Add the comparison queries — "[your category] vs [alternative]" — and the "who is the best" queries that are common in your vertical.

**Test across all three major AI surfaces.** Run each query in ChatGPT (GPT-4), Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews. Record the results in a spreadsheet: query, AI engine, domain cited, page URL if visible, and a brief note on how the citation was framed. Some AI engines will show citations explicitly (Perplexity always shows source links); others require you to ask for sources.

**Look for patterns in who appears.** After testing twenty or more queries, patterns will emerge. Three or four competitors will appear repeatedly across multiple queries and multiple AI engines. These are the businesses that have systematically solved AI visibility for your category. Focus your structural analysis on these repeat winners — they represent the competitive ceiling you are trying to reach.

**Note absence patterns for your own site.** Document every query where a competitor appears and your site does not. This absence list is the input for gap analysis in Step 3.

## Step 2: Analyze what content structure gets competitors cited

Once you have identified which competitor pages are being cited, pull up those pages and analyze them against the six core citation signals. This is the core of the competitive analysis — you are building a detailed profile of what a highly citable page in your category looks like.

**Content structure and answer format.** Cited pages almost always answer the primary question directly in the first two to three paragraphs, then expand with supporting detail. AI engines are trained to find the most direct answer to a query — pages that bury the answer after a lengthy preamble are less reliably cited than pages where the answer is visible immediately. Check whether cited competitor pages use a "quick answer" or "summary" section near the top. Check whether they use definition-first writing (state the concept, then explain it) rather than narrative-first writing. Check whether they use headers that match query language precisely — a page titled "How to Calculate Customer Acquisition Cost" with an H2 that says "What is customer acquisition cost?" maps directly to query patterns.

**Schema implementation.** View the page source or use a schema validator to check what schema the cited competitor has implemented. FAQ schema on informational pages is the most common gap — it creates machine-readable question-and-answer pairs that AI engines can extract directly. HowTo schema on procedural content, Article schema with authorship attributes, and Organization schema with verifiable credentials are the other high-value implementations. Record what schema types each cited competitor has deployed.

**Author signals and E-E-A-T.** Check the author attribution on each cited page. Is there a named author with a linked bio page? Does the bio page include credentials, publications, professional affiliations, or other expertise signals? AI engines that are calibrated to weight experience and expertise will preferentially cite content where authorship is clear and credentials are verifiable. A page that lists "Staff Writer" as the author with no bio link is less citable than a page that credits a named expert with ten years of documented industry experience.

**Content depth and coverage.** Compare word count, but more importantly compare concept coverage. Does the cited competitor page cover the topic more completely than your equivalent page? Does it include data, examples, counterarguments, or FAQs that your page omits? Depth of coverage is a proxy for expertise that AI engines can measure — a page that covers more facets of a topic with more specific detail signals authoritative knowledge.

## Step 3: Identify structural gaps versus your own pages

Gap analysis is a direct comparison: take each dimension you scored for the cited competitor page, score your equivalent page on the same dimension, and record the delta. The goal is to produce a ranked list of gaps sorted by the combination of citation impact and implementation effort.

**High-impact, low-effort gaps** are the priority. FAQ schema on an existing page that already contains FAQ-formatted content is a few hours of implementation work. Adding author attribution with a linked bio page to pages that currently show no author is a content task rather than a technical task. Rewriting the introduction of a page to lead with a direct answer is a single editing session. These changes can produce measurable citation improvement within four to six weeks.

**High-impact, high-effort gaps** are the medium-term projects. A competitor that has published a comprehensive guide covering fifty questions about your topic, while your equivalent page covers fifteen, represents a content depth gap that requires substantive writing work to close. A competitor whose author has a well-developed web presence with published articles, speaking credits, and social proof represents an E-E-A-T gap that requires ongoing investment over months.

**Low-impact gaps** belong at the bottom of the list regardless of effort. If a cited competitor has a slightly higher domain authority due to backlink volume, closing that gap through link building is a multi-month effort with diffuse citation impact — other gaps will produce faster results with less investment.

## Schema gap analysis: what cited competitors have that you don't

Schema is one of the most actionable dimensions of competitive analysis because gaps are binary and closures are implementable. Either a page has FAQ schema or it does not. Either the Organization markup includes sameAs links to verifiable external profiles or it does not. The schema gap analysis is a checklist comparison that produces a concrete implementation list.

Run each cited competitor page through Google's Rich Results Test and the Schema Markup Validator. Record every schema type present. Common patterns among highly cited pages in most content categories include: FAQPage schema on any page with a question-and-answer structure; Article or BlogPosting schema with datePublished, dateModified, and author attributes; Person schema on author bio pages with the same name, credentials, and sameAs links that appear in Article authorship; and BreadcrumbList schema for navigational context.

Compare this inventory against your own pages on equivalent topics. Every schema type that cited competitors have and you do not is a gap with a defined implementation path. Prioritize schema types that appear on the majority of cited competitor pages — if four out of five cited competitors have FAQ schema on their how-to content, that is a strong signal that FAQ schema is a citation driver in your category.

According to [Semrush's guide to structured data](https://www.semrush.com/blog/structured-data/), implementing relevant schema markup can improve how search engines and AI tools understand and surface content. The correlation between FAQ schema presence and AI citation frequency is particularly strong for informational content because FAQ schema creates exactly the question-answer pairs that AI engines use to construct direct answers.

## Content depth comparison: their coverage versus yours

Depth comparison goes beyond word count. The goal is to identify specific concepts, questions, or facets of a topic that a cited competitor covers and your equivalent page omits. This requires reading both pages carefully — not scanning for length, but mapping the conceptual territory each one covers.

A useful technique: use a competitor's cited page to generate a list of every distinct question or subtopic it addresses, then do the same for your equivalent page, then identify the items on the competitor's list that are absent from yours. Each item on the gap list is a section you could add, a paragraph you could expand, or an example you could include.

Depth gaps often cluster around three types of content that AI engines reliably cite: specific data and statistics (AI engines prefer concrete numbers over vague claims), worked examples (showing the principle applied to a real situation increases both depth and citeability), and counterargument handling (pages that address objections and alternative views demonstrate more complete expertise than pages that present only the favorable case).

For queries where your competitors are cited and you are not, depth is frequently the deciding factor after schema is accounted for. A page with perfect schema but thin content will lose to a page with decent schema and comprehensive coverage.

## Building a competitor citation matrix

The citation matrix is the central deliverable of the competitive analysis. It is a structured comparison table that shows, for each competitor, how they score on the six core AI citation signals: Schema Implementation, Content Depth, Author E-E-A-T, Review or Social Proof Count, Domain Authority, and GBP or Off-Site Completeness. Your own site appears in the matrix as the baseline row — this makes gaps immediately visible.

Score each cell on a three-level scale: strong, moderate, or weak. Use color coding to make the pattern visible at a glance. A competitor row that shows strong scores across four of six dimensions and weak scores on two tells you something specific: the two weak dimensions are not what is driving their citations. The four strong dimensions are the ones worth reverse-engineering.

Your baseline row will typically show a mix of scores. The cells where competitors are strong and you are weak — particularly when those cells align with dimensions that correlate with citation in your category — are your prioritized action items.

## From matrix to action plan

The matrix makes prioritization visual, but the action plan requires one more step: ranking gaps by the combination of citation frequency impact and implementation timeline. Not all strong-vs-weak gaps are equal. A schema gap can typically be closed in days. A content depth gap requires weeks. An author E-E-A-T gap requires months of sustained activity. Rank your gap list accordingly and build a sprint-based execution schedule.

**Sprint one (weeks one to two):** Close all schema gaps. Add FAQ schema to every page where you have Q&A content. Add Article schema with author attribution to every content page. Implement Organization schema with sameAs links on your homepage and About page. These are implementation tasks that do not require new content — they add machine-readability to content that already exists.

**Sprint two (weeks three to six):** Close content depth gaps on the five to ten pages most likely to win citation if improved. Use the competitor page as a map of the topic territory, write to cover what is missing, and add specific data, examples, and FAQ sections that cited competitor pages include. This is where the quality of the competitive analysis pays off most directly — you are not guessing what to add, you are adding what the citation data shows is working.

**Sprint three (months two to four):** Close author E-E-A-T gaps. Build author bio pages with full credentials. Earn bylines on external publications in your category. Connect author schema to external profiles on LinkedIn, recognized industry publications, and speaker profiles where applicable. According to [Ahrefs' research on E-E-A-T signals](https://ahrefs.com/blog/eeat/), demonstrable expertise and verifiable authorship are increasingly weighted in content quality assessments — this applies directly to how AI engines evaluate citation-worthiness.

Track citation changes against baseline throughout execution using the measurement framework from the [how to get cited by AI search systems guide](/blog/how-to-get-cited-by-ai-search-systems/). Every four weeks, re-run the query tests from Step 1 and update the matrix. Gaps that have been closed should show citation improvement. If a gap closure does not produce citation improvement within two testing cycles, it may indicate that another gap is the binding constraint — update your prioritization accordingly.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many competitors should I include in my AI competitive analysis?

Focus on three to five competitors who appear most frequently in your query testing results. Including too many competitors dilutes the analysis — you end up with a large dataset but less clarity on what specifically drives citations. The competitors worth studying in depth are those who consistently appear across multiple queries and multiple AI engines, because their citation patterns reflect systematic optimization rather than random AI outputs. If you find more than five consistent performers, tier them: analyze the top two or three deeply and note the others for reference.

### How often should I refresh the competitor citation matrix?

Refresh the full matrix every sixty to ninety days, and run lightweight query spot-checks every two to four weeks. AI citation patterns can shift when AI engines update their models, when a competitor significantly improves their content, or when your own optimizations take effect. Monthly spot-checks keep you aware of major shifts without the overhead of a full analysis every cycle. The full refresh is worth the time investment once per quarter because it resets priorities based on current data rather than assumptions.

### Does a competitor's domain authority determine their AI citation advantage?

Domain authority is one of six citation signals, not the primary one. Competitors with lower domain authority than yours frequently outperform you on AI citations because they have stronger schema implementation, better-structured content, or clearer author credentials. Domain authority is a lagging indicator that is slow to change and difficult to target directly. Focus first on the signals you can change quickly — schema, content structure, author attribution — and treat domain authority as a long-term project that compounds alongside the faster wins.

### Can I use AI tools to help with the competitive analysis itself?

Yes, with important caveats. AI tools are useful for analyzing competitor page structure, identifying schema gaps, and summarizing content coverage. You can paste a competitor page into ChatGPT or Claude and ask it to list every distinct question or subtopic the page addresses, which accelerates the depth comparison step considerably. However, do not use AI tools to run the query citation tests — you need to see the actual AI engine outputs for your target queries, not a simulation of them. The query testing step requires using the live AI engines directly to capture real citation data.

### What is the fastest single change that improves AI citation rates after competitive analysis?

FAQ schema on existing content pages is consistently the highest-impact, lowest-effort change that competitive analysis reveals. Most pages that answer questions in paragraph form can have FAQ schema added in a few hours without rewriting the content — the schema simply marks up the question-answer structure that already exists. Because FAQ schema creates machine-readable Q&A pairs that AI engines extract directly, adding it to a page that already has strong content often produces measurable citation improvement within four to six weeks of indexing.

### How do I analyze competitor AI citation signals if they do not have a direct equivalent page to mine?

When a cited competitor does not have a direct equivalent to your page, analyze their site structure rather than a specific page. Look at how they organize their content — do they have pillar pages with hub-and-spoke supporting content? Do they cluster related topics under a single category landing page? The structural pattern that produces citations often matters as much as any individual page. Also examine their schema at the site level: the Organization, Person, and WebSite schema that covers the whole domain is a foundational signal that benefits all their pages, and it is worth implementing regardless of whether a specific page-to-page comparison is available.
