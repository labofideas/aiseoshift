Neither wins on length, because AI search doesn't cite pages. It cites passages.

Ahrefs analyzed 174,048 pages and 1.6 million cited URLs and found the correlation between word count and AI Overview citations is 0.04, effectively zero, with 53.4% of all citations going to pages under 1,000 words. What separates cited pages from ignored ones is whether any single section delivers a complete, self-contained answer in roughly 100 to 200 words. A 600-word page built as three tight answers beats a 4,000-word page built as one continuous essay.

The rest of this article shows the evidence, the one place long-form still wins, and the structure that captures both.

## What the Research Actually Shows

Since we first published this comparison, several large-scale studies have replaced educated guesses with data. Here's what holds up.

### Word count barely predicts Google AI citations

The Ahrefs study is the largest public dataset on this question: across 1.6 million cited URLs, word count and citation probability are essentially uncorrelated (0.04). More striking, pages in the top citation slot averaged just 1,270 words, while pages in citation positions four through ten averaged 1,690. The most-cited sources were shorter than the also-rans.

That inverts the old SEO instinct that comprehensiveness wins by default. Google's AI surfaces are assembling an answer from fragments, and a focused page gives the retrieval system a cleaner fragment.

### But ChatGPT leans the other way

SE Ranking's study of 129,000 domains found pages over 2,900 words earned 59% more ChatGPT citations than pages under 800 words. Different engine, different retrieval behavior: ChatGPT's browsing tends to favor sources that cover a topic broadly enough to support multi-part answers.

The same study contains the finding that matters most, though, and it's about structure rather than length: **pages with 120 to 180 words between H2/H3 headings received 70% more ChatGPT citations than pages with thin sections under 50 words.** Section sizing beat page sizing.

### The grounding budget explains everything

Research by Dan Petrovic (DEJAN) on Google's grounding behavior found the AI works with a budget of roughly 2,000 words per query across all sources, extracting about 531 words from the top-cited page in snippets averaging just 15.5 words each.

Read that again. The machine consuming your 4,000-word guide is pulling 15-word fragments against a 2,000-word total budget. Length doesn't buy you more of the answer. Extractable, quotable sentences do.

## So When Does Long-Form Still Win?

Three situations, all real:

1. **Multi-intent topics.** A comparison or buying guide serves "what is it," "A vs B," and "which should I pick" in one URL. Each intent is a separately citable passage; the page earns citations across many prompts. This is depth as *coverage*, not depth as *length*.
2. **ChatGPT-weighted niches.** If your audience researches in ChatGPT (developers, marketers, B2B buyers), the 59% depth advantage is worth chasing, combined with the 120 to 180 word section discipline that actually drove citations in that dataset.
3. **Topical authority.** Engines cite sources they've seen answer adjacent questions well. A substantial pillar supported by focused satellite pages builds the entity-level trust that gets your *short* pages cited too. Our [AI citation rate research](/blog/ai-citation-rate-research-2026/) found exactly this pattern across content types.

Short-form wins for definitions, news and timely updates (freshness beats depth on fast-moving queries), single procedures, and local or business facts. If the honest answer fits in 400 words, padding it to 1,500 actively hurts: filler dilutes the extractable passage the engine came for.

## The Structure That Wins on Both: The 3-Layer Model

Our recommendation hasn't changed since the first version of this article. The new research just explains why it works.

**Layer 1: Direct answer (50 to 200 words).** Open the page by fully answering the core question. This is the passage Google's extraction favors; independent SERP analyses consistently find extracted passages cluster around 100 to 300 words. Write this layer as if it's the only thing the AI will read, because usually it is.

**Layer 2: Supporting evidence (500 to 1,000 words).** Data, reasoning, and the sub-questions a serious reader asks next. Keep every H2/H3 section in the 120 to 180 word band: self-contained, quotable, one idea per section.

**Layer 3: Depth on demand (only if the topic earns it).** Edge cases, worked examples, FAQ. This layer serves ChatGPT-style comprehensive retrieval and human buyers. Add it when the topic has genuine remaining questions, never to hit a word count.

A definition page stops after Layer 1. A how-to stops after Layer 2. A comparison or pillar runs all three. Length becomes an *output* of the topic's real complexity instead of a target. ## Length Targets by Content Type

Based on AI citation patterns and query intent, practical targets:

| Content type | Target length | Key structural requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How-to post (simple process) | 600–1,000w | Numbered steps, clear headings |
| How-to guide (complex process) | 1,500–2,500w | Numbered steps + edge cases + FAQ |
| Service page | 400–800w | Clear offer, FAQ, structured data |
| Comparison post (2 options) | 1,200–2,000w | Comparison table, decision criteria |
| Comparison post (3+ options) | 2,000–3,500w | Multi-column table, use case sections |
| News post | 400–700w | Dateline, direct summary, context |
| Pillar page | 3,000–6,000w | Full sub-topic coverage, internal links |
| FAQ page | 800–2,000w | FAQPage schema, 8–15 questions |
| Definition / glossary | 300–600w | Direct definition, examples, related terms |

These are targets, not ceilings. The right length is the length the topic requires, no more, no less. If a comparison post only needs 900 words to be genuinely comprehensive, it should be 900 words.

## Why AI Systems Penalize Padding

AI systems have become effective at distinguishing substantive content from padded content, and the citation data reflects it.

Padding patterns that reduce citation probability: restating the same point in different words across multiple sections, opening paragraphs that summarize what the article will cover instead of delivering value, transition sections that add no information, and generic closing sections that restate the intro.

A tight 800-word post with a strong direct answer, three substantive supporting points, and a concise FAQ will out-cite a bloated 3,000-word post that pads its way to length. AI systems extract from the most information-dense passages. Pages full of filler give them less to extract from, and a weaker authority signal.

The test: if you removed any given paragraph and the page lost no meaning, that paragraph should be cut.

## What to Do With Your Existing Content

- **Long pages that never get cited:** don't shorten them. Restructure them. Add a Layer-1 direct answer at the top, break wall-of-text sections into 120 to 180 word units under specific headings, and give each section a heading that states the question it answers.
- **Short pages that never get cited:** check whether the answer is complete and self-contained in the first 100 words. Thin pages fail not because they're short but because they're partial.
