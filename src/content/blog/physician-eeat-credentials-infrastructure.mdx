Google classifies medical content as the most rigorous YMYL category. AI engines inherited and intensified those filters. Anonymous medical content does not get cited. Generic "our team of doctors" bylines do not pass extraction. The practices recommended by ChatGPT and surfaced in Google AI Overviews are the ones with full credentialed physician infrastructure.

This is the build. Six elements, implementable in a focused 30-day sprint.

## Element 1: Named physician bylines

Every clinical page needs a named physician author. Not "Our Team," not "Editorial Staff," not the practice name. A specific physician with verifiable credentials.

The byline appears:

- At the top of every clinical page
- Linked to a dedicated physician bio page
- With credentials suffix (MD, DO, NP-BC, PA-C)

Example: "By Sarah Johnson, MD · Board-certified in Internal Medicine · Updated May 27, 2026"

For non-physician-authored content (administrative, patient education by allied health professionals), surface both author and reviewing physician: "By Lisa Martinez, RN · Reviewed by Dr. Sarah Johnson, MD"

## Element 2: Full physician bio pages

Every physician byline links to a dedicated bio page. The bio is not a paragraph, it is a substantive page that signals expertise.

The required elements:

- Professional photo
- Full name and credentials
- Medical specialty and sub-specialties
- Board certifications with year obtained
- State medical license(s) and license number(s)
- Education and training
- Years in practice
- Hospital affiliations
- Publications, presentations, speaking engagements
- Languages spoken
- Patient demographics served
- Treatment philosophy
- Links to content authored by this physician

The bio page is the canonical location for `Person` schema with `hasCredential` markup.

## Element 3: Credential verification

AI engines cross-reference claimed credentials against public databases (state medical boards, ABMS, AOA). Make verification easy:

- License number visible
- Link to the state medical board's lookup
- ABMS board certification displayed
- Hospital affiliations linked or verifiable

If a physician holds multiple state licenses, list all. If multiple board certifications, list all. More verifiable credentials produce stronger extraction.

## Element 4: Specialty depth and topical authority

E-E-A-T is partly claimed expertise and partly demonstrated authority. A physician claiming cardiology expertise should have 10 to 30 published pieces on cardiology topics under their byline.

Build topical authority deliberately:

- Each physician picks 2 to 4 sub-specialty areas
- Each sub-specialty area gets a content map of 10 to 20 sub-topics
- Each sub-topic gets a published piece under that physician's byline
- The physician's bio page links to the content cluster

When AI engines query "cardiologist for AFib in [city]," they extract named physicians with demonstrated depth on AFib.

## Element 5: Content currency and review

Medical knowledge changes. Content with a 2022 date and no review since reads as outdated. The fix:

- Publication date on every clinical page
- Updated date when content is reviewed and revised
- Quarterly review cycle for time-sensitive content (treatment guidelines, dosing, new approvals)
- Annual review for evergreen content
- Visible "Last reviewed by Dr. [Name] on [Date]" notation

AI engines weigh recency heavily for YMYL medical content. A piece reviewed in the last 12 months by a credentialed physician carries dramatically more weight than a piece untouched in three years.

## Element 6: Citations and references

Substantive medical content cites authoritative sources:

- Peer-reviewed journals
- Clinical practice guidelines (AHA, ADA, USPSTF, etc.)
- FDA approvals and labeling
- CDC, NIH, and government health agency resources
- Society position statements

Inline citations with links to primary sources signal scholarly rigor. Content without citations gets filtered more aggressively in YMYL extraction.

## Element 7: Disclosures

YMYL medical content needs disclosures:

- "This article is for general information and does not constitute medical advice."
- "Consult your physician for advice specific to your situation."
- "Information current as of [date]. Medical guidelines change."
- Conflict of interest disclosures if applicable

Proper disclosures signal trustworthiness and reduce regulatory exposure.

## The 30-day build

A realistic sprint for a practice starting from generic content:

- **Week 1:** Audit existing content for byline status. Catalog physicians and their specialty areas.
- **Week 2:** Build bio pages for each physician with full credentials and schema.
- **Week 3:** Backfill bylines on existing clinical content. Surface "Last reviewed by" notation.
- **Week 4:** Citation audit on top 20 clinical pages. Add primary source links.

Twelve weeks in, the practice has complete E-E-A-T infrastructure ready for ongoing content production.

## The compounding effect

A practice with three years of physician-authored content under named bylines outranks practices with five years of anonymous content. The gap widens as AI engines weigh historical attribution more heavily.

## FAQs

### What if some staff are PAs or NPs, not MDs?

PAs and NPs can byline content with their accurate credentials. Their `hasCredential` schema should reflect their actual licensure. Both are valid YMYL contributors when properly credentialed.

### Can I use freelance physician authors?

Yes, with full byline attribution. The physician's bio page should disclose the contractor relationship. The physician must be willing to be publicly associated with the practice.

### How do I handle content by non-clinical staff?

Two acceptable patterns: (1) byline the non-clinical author and add "Reviewed by Dr. [Name]" notation, or (2) for technical clinical content, byline the physician who reviewed and approved.

### What about ghostwriting?

The named physician must have substantive involvement, direction, review, fact-checking, final approval. Pure ghostwriting where the physician has not engaged with the content is risky.

### Should partners or senior physicians be the primary bylined authors?

Senior physicians make the strongest bylines because credentials are most verifiable and experience is deepest. But every credentialed physician on staff can and should byline in their specialty.
