Generative Engine Optimization, or GEO, is the practice of shaping content so AI systems that generate answers, rather than just list links, retrieve it, use it in their synthesis, and cite it as a source. The term comes from a specific 2023 research paper, not a marketing department, and it names something SEO and AEO don't quite cover: the process of an AI system blending multiple web sources into one generated answer.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - GEO comes from a real academic paper (Aggarwal et al., 2023, KDD 2024), which reported that its tested optimization methods boosted visibility in generative engine responses by up to 40%, with effectiveness varying by domain.
> - GEO doesn't replace SEO. Crawlability, indexing, and structured data are prerequisites; GEO adds a layer on top focused on source selection during AI synthesis.
> - A verified 2026 Ahrefs analysis found the overlap between AI Overview citations and top-10 organic rankings has fallen to roughly 38% (37.1% organic-only), down from about 76% in mid-2025, meaning strong rankings alone no longer guarantee AI citation.
> - The clearest wins come from narrow-focus pages, verifiable claims, extractable structure, and a current publish or update date, not from gaming any single AI platform.
> - There's no single GEO dashboard. Measuring it means running your own queries through AI systems and watching platform-level visibility tools where they exist.

## Where the term actually comes from

GEO isn't an SEO agency's rebrand of an old idea. It's the name given in a specific paper: ["GEO: Generative Engine Optimization"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735) by Pranjal Aggarwal, Vishvak Murahari, Tanmay Rajpurohit, Ashwin Kalyan, Karthik Narasimhan, and Ameet Deshpande, first posted to arXiv in November 2023 and accepted to KDD 2024. The paper's own framing is direct about why it exists:

> "Given the black-box and fast-moving nature of generative engines, content creators have little to no control over when and how their content is displayed. With generative engines here to stay, we must ensure the creator economy is not disadvantaged."

The authors introduced GEO as "a flexible black-box optimization framework for optimizing and defining visibility metrics," along with GEO-bench, a benchmark of real user queries across multiple domains used to test which content strategies actually move the needle. Their headline finding: the methods they tested **boosted visibility in generative engine responses by up to 40%**, though "the efficacy of these strategies varies across domains, underscoring the need for domain-specific optimization methods."

That's worth sitting with. This is one research paper's benchmark result, not an industry-wide guarantee, and the paper itself flags that no single tactic works equally well everywhere. Anyone citing "GEO boosts visibility by 40%" as a universal promise is overstating a domain-varying benchmark finding.

## GEO vs SEO vs AEO: how they relate

| Discipline | Primary focus | Key question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SEO | Organic ranking in search engines | Does my page rank for relevant queries? |
| AEO | Performance in answer-first surfaces | Is my content selected as the direct answer? |
| GEO | Inclusion in AI-generated responses | Is my content retrieved, synthesized, and cited by generative systems? |

A page that performs well for GEO usually performs reasonably well for SEO and AEO too, because the underlying quality signals overlap. The emphasis differs: SEO optimizes for crawlability and relevance, AEO optimizes for answer clarity, and GEO optimizes for whether an AI system chooses your content as one of the sources it blends into a generated response.

**SEO gets your page into the index. AEO gets your answer displayed. GEO gets your content woven into the generated response and attributed as a source.** That's the clearest one-line distinction, and it's worth quoting back if someone asks you to explain the difference in a meeting.

## What gets cited, based on what's actually been measured

The honest state of GEO research in 2026 is narrower than the volume of content written about it suggests. Two things are independently verifiable right now: the arXiv paper's benchmark result above, and a shift in how often AI citations overlap with top-10 organic rankings.

A 2026 Ahrefs analysis of roughly 863,000 keyword SERPs and about 4 million AI Overview URLs, [reported by ALM Corp](https://almcorp.com/blog/google-ai-overview-citations-drop-top-ranking-pages-2026/), found that only 38% of pages cited in Google AI Overviews also ranked in the top 10 organically for the same query (37.1% when filtered to organic-only listings, excluding ads and featured snippets). That's a sharp drop from roughly 76% in mid-2025, by the same report's account. A separate BrightEdge analysis cited in the same piece found an even lower overlap, around 17%, using different methodology, which is itself a useful reminder that these numbers move depending on who's measuring and how.

The practical takeaway isn't a specific percentage you should memorize. It's that the correlation between "ranks well" and "gets cited by an AI Overview" has weakened measurably over roughly a year, in multiple independent analyses. Content that would have been safe to leave alone in 2025, because it ranked, can no longer assume a top-10 position means AI visibility too.

Beyond that specific data point, Google's own guidance for [AI features in Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) states the company's intent is to surface links that help people explore further, which only works if a page's claims can be confirmed against its own structured data and visible content. That's a statement of intent from Google, not a disclosed ranking formula, and it's worth treating it as exactly that level of certainty: directional, not a mechanism you can reverse-engineer.

## What GEO tries to influence

A GEO program works across four stages of the generative pipeline, and none of them are measurable through a single metric:

### 1. Retrieval probability

Can the AI system find your content when it searches for sources at all? This depends on indexing, topical relevance, and whether your content sits within a [topic cluster](/blog/build-a-topic-cluster/) that signals depth rather than a single isolated page.

### 2. Source selection

When the system evaluates multiple candidate pages, does it pick yours? This depends on how completely the page addresses the query and how easily the information can be extracted and verified, not on backlink count alone.

### 3. Citation likelihood

Does the system attribute the information to your page specifically, rather than folding it into an uncredited summary? Clear structure and verifiable claims help here, but no platform has published an exact citation-attribution formula.

### 4. Brand recall

Does the person leave the interaction remembering your brand, even without clicking through? This depends on naming consistency and repeated topic ownership across the pages a system might draw from.

## How generative systems tend to select sources

The exact mechanisms differ by platform and are not fully public, but the pages that consistently perform well in generative environments share recognizable traits, consistent with both the GEO paper's framing and Google's own content guidance:

- **Clear topical focus.** Pages that try to cover everything about a topic superficially tend to lose to pages that cover one specific angle thoroughly, because generative systems decompose broad queries into sub-queries that reward narrow, complete answers.
- **Direct answers positioned early.** An extractable answer in the first few hundred words is easier for a system to pull than one buried three sections down.
- **Verifiable, specific claims.** Statements backed by named data, dates, and sources are easier to trust and cite than unsupported generalizations. This is the same discipline behind [information gain optimization](/blog/information-gain-optimization/): content that adds something a system hasn't already seen elsewhere.
- **Extractable structure.** Short paragraphs, descriptive headings, and tables make content easier to parse and cite than dense unbroken text.
- **Current publish or update dates.** A comprehensive page from two years ago that hasn't been touched tends to lose ground to a less comprehensive but current one.

## Where the term gets discussed, and misused

GEO shows up constantly in SEO practitioner communities, including threads on Reddit's SEO forums, where the debate usually splits into two camps: people treating it as a genuinely new discipline worth separate tracking, and people arguing it's just SEO with an AI-era rebrand. Both positions have a point. The underlying quality signals overlap heavily with good SEO, but the specific question GEO asks, whether an AI system picks your page as a source during synthesis, is a real and separately measurable thing even if it rides on the same content fundamentals.

That same split shows up in how GEO *tools* are reviewed. Several platforms now track AI citation visibility specifically, and [comparing how they measure it](/blog/otterly-vs-peec-vs-profound-vs-scrunch/) is a useful way to see how differently vendors define "a citation" in the first place, since there's no single agreed definition across the industry yet.

## GEO principles that hold up

**Publish pages with a single dominant job.** The most citable pages answer one primary question well rather than spreading thin across several adjacent topics in one URL.

**Make claims easy to verify.** Name the data source, the sample size, and the specific finding. "Studies show" without a named study is weaker, and less citable, than naming exactly which study and what it found.

**Strengthen your entity footprint.** Consistent naming for your brand and authors across your site and the wider web, backed by structured data, helps systems recognize who's behind the content they might cite.

**Build clusters, not isolated pages.** A single strong page is more citable sitting inside a cluster of related pages than standing alone, because it signals depth rather than a one-off.

**Refresh on a real schedule.** AI systems and the studies measuring them both favor current content. [Refreshing older content strategically](/blog/how-to-refresh-old-seo-content/), rather than leaving a comprehensive but stale guide untouched, is worth building into an editorial calendar rather than treating as a one-time project.

## Where teams confuse GEO

**Treating GEO as a prompt-engineering exercise.** Running test prompts through ChatGPT or Perplexity to see what comes back is a reasonable way to check your current visibility. It is not the same as doing GEO, which happens at the site and content level, not inside a prompt box.

**Thinking GEO replaces SEO.** It extends it. A page that can't be crawled and indexed will never be retrieved by a generative system in the first place, full stop.

**Optimizing for one platform only.** ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's AI Overviews don't select sources identically, and chasing one platform's specific quirks at the expense of general content quality tends to age badly as these systems keep changing.

**Confusing a mention with a citation.** Being named inside a generated answer without a link or clear attribution signals some brand recognition, but it isn't the same as earning a source-level citation that credits your page specifically.

## A practical GEO checklist

Before publishing or refreshing a page, check it against these:

- [ ] Is the page narrowly focused on one user need?
- [ ] Is the direct answer visible within the first few hundred words?
- [ ] Does the page contain original data, analysis, or first-hand perspective?
- [ ] Are claims specific, evidence-backed, and attributable to a named source?
- [ ] Is the page structured with extractable headings, lists, and tables?
- [ ] Is the page connected to a broader topic cluster through internal links?
- [ ] Would a system reading only this page know who published it and why they're credible?
- [ ] Has the content been updated within the last 6 months?

## The bottom line

GEO is a real, specifically sourced research framework, not an invented buzzword, and the arXiv paper behind it reports a genuine (if domain-dependent) visibility benefit from the strategies it tested. What's changed by 2026 is the data showing how much the ground has shifted underneath it: strong organic rankings alone no longer reliably predict AI citation, based on independent analyses showing that overlap falling from roughly 76% to under 40% in about a year. The response isn't chasing a single platform's algorithm. It's doing the same work that's always separated useful content from filler, narrow focus, verifiable claims, extractable structure, and a current update date, with the understanding that an AI system choosing sources is now a separate, real audience worth writing for.
