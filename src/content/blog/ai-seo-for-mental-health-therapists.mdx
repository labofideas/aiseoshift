Demand for mental health services has climbed every year since 2020. The [American Psychological Association](https://www.apa.org/) reports that more than 40% of psychologists have no openings for new patients, yet millions of people who need help still can't find a therapist they trust. The mismatch isn't shortage alone. It's discoverability.

In 2026, when someone works up the courage to search for a therapist, the journey starts with AI. "What's the difference between a therapist and a psychologist?" "What type of therapy works for PTSD?" "Does my insurance cover mental health in [city]?" Google AI Overviews answer these questions, and the practices cited in those answers get the appointment requests.

Most therapist websites are invisible to AI engines. Thin bios, generic "we treat anxiety and depression" service pages, and no structured data mean that even the most clinically excellent practice doesn't show up when it matters most. This playbook changes that.

## Quick answer

Four things drive therapy practice visibility in 2026: dedicated pages for every specialty, condition, and modality you offer; clear content about insurance, fees, and telehealth availability; individual therapist profile pages anchored by real credentials and clinical training; and structured schema markup that tells AI engines exactly what kind of provider you are and where you practice. A Psychology Today profile is a starting point, your own website needs to be the authority. ## Why mental health SEO is uniquely challenging

Mental health content sits at the apex of Google's YMYL (Your Money or Your Life) hierarchy. Medical content is high-stakes. Mental health content carries additional layers: people searching for a therapist are often in crisis, navigating stigma, and making deeply personal decisions about who to trust with their inner life.

Three specific challenges set therapy practices apart from almost every other local service:

**YMYL at maximum sensitivity.** Queries like "therapist for suicidal thoughts [city]" or "trauma therapist who specializes in sexual abuse" trigger the highest scrutiny Google applies to any content. Anonymous websites, generic copy, and unverified credentials are filtered out entirely. AI engines will not cite a source they can't verify.

**No before/after photography.** Other health verticals use clinical outcome imagery to build credibility signals. Therapy cannot. The trust signals available to therapists are text-based: credentials, training, named bylines, published writing, and the depth and specificity of clinical content.

**Reviews are structurally complicated.** A dentist can ask patients to leave a Google review. A therapist navigates HIPAA constraints, therapeutic relationship ethics, and the reality that a client leaving a public review implicitly discloses they are in therapy. Reviews still matter for local SEO, but the approach requires care. We address this separately below.

The path forward is investing deeply in the signals that remain: credentials, specificity, schema, and local authority. Our post on [E-E-A-T for YMYL content](/seo/eeat/) provides the foundational framework.

## Pillar 1: Specialty and modality pages

The single highest-leverage content investment for a therapy practice. Most therapist websites have one service page that says something like "I specialize in anxiety, depression, trauma, and relationship issues." That sentence cannot rank for anything. Each of those is a separate searcher intent, a separate content topic, and a separate AI citation opportunity.

**Condition-specific pages to build:**

- Anxiety therapy (generalized anxiety, social anxiety, panic disorder as sub-pages if volume supports it)
- Depression therapy
- PTSD and trauma therapy
- OCD (Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder)
- Eating disorders and body image
- Grief and loss
- Life transitions and adjustment
- Couples and relationship therapy
- LGBTQ+ affirming therapy

**Modality-specific pages to build:**

- Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)
- EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing)
- Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)
- Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)
- Somatic therapy and body-based approaches
- Trauma-Focused CBT
- Psychodynamic therapy

Each page should explain what the condition or modality is in plain language, describe your clinical approach to treating it, clarify who is a good fit for this type of work, and answer the practical questions a new client would ask. A person searching "EMDR therapist [city]" wants to know what EMDR actually involves, whether it works for their situation, and what your specific experience with it is, not a marketing paragraph about your "warm and collaborative approach."

Modality pages also serve as natural AI citation candidates. When someone asks ChatGPT "how does DBT work for borderline personality disorder," AI engines look for clear, clinically grounded explanations. If your DBT page provides that explanation better than any other local source, it becomes a candidate for a cited recommendation when that same person follows up with "DBT therapist in [city]."

Population-specific pages add another layer: therapy for teens and adolescents, therapy for adults in midlife transitions, therapy for BIPOC clients, culturally responsive therapy. These pages signal both clinical focus and therapeutic fit.

## Pillar 2: Insurance and access content

When someone has worked up the courage to search for a therapist, practical barriers, cost, insurance, availability, are the reason they don't follow through. Content that directly addresses these barriers converts searchers into patients and signals your practice to AI engines answering "therapists who take [insurance carrier] in [city]."

**What to publish:**

A dedicated insurance page listing every in-network carrier you accept. Do not bury this in a FAQ or a footer. Carriers change networks; update this page at least quarterly and add a "last verified" date. Searchers, and AI engines, use this to filter.

A clear fees and sliding scale page. If you offer sliding scale, explain the range, the income criteria if any, and how to inquire. People in financial stress need to know this before they call. Therapists who are transparent about fees convert better and serve a broader community.

A telehealth availability page. Many clients prefer or require telehealth, distance, disability, anxiety about in-person appointments, rural location. Specify which states you are licensed in for telehealth (this matters legally and for search, someone in a neighboring state cannot work with you regardless of platform), what platforms you use, and whether telehealth is available for all presenting concerns or specific ones.

An accepting new patients page or a clear section on your homepage. Something as simple as "Currently accepting new clients, [contact/booking link]" signals availability to both searchers and AI engines. Practices that are on waitlists should say so honestly and offer an intake form to join.

This content also directly targets long-tail AI queries: "does [insurance carrier] cover online therapy," "how much does therapy cost without insurance in [city]," "therapists accepting new patients [city]." ## Pillar 3: Therapist profile pages

For a group practice, individual therapist profiles are the most underbuilt asset. Most group practice websites have a "Meet Our Team" page with headshots and 3-sentence bios. This does not establish E-E-A-T. It does not rank. It does not get cited.

Each therapist needs a dedicated profile page that functions as a professional credential document. This means:

**Credentials and licensure up front.** License type (LCSW, PhD, PsyD, LPC, LMFT, etc.), license number if your state requires or permits public display, state of licensure, and year licensed. This information should be in the page body text, not just an image. Schema markup makes it machine-readable.

**Education and clinical training.** Graduate program, degree, year conferred. Post-graduate training, residencies, and supervised hours. For psychologists, internship and postdoctoral fellowship placements. Specific EMDR certification, DBT intensive training, trauma-focused CBT training, these are credential signals, not just marketing copy.

**Clinical approach.** A genuine, specific description of how this therapist works, not "client-centered, collaborative, and holistic." What theoretical framework guides their work? What does a first session typically look like? What kinds of problems do they find most meaningful to work on?

**Who they work with.** Age ranges, presenting concerns, populations. Is this therapist taking couples? Teenagers only? Adults who have experienced complex trauma? Specificity here serves both SEO (it matches longer-tail queries) and client fit (the right clients self-select).

**Professional contributions.** Published articles, podcast appearances, conference presentations, guest posts, community training, any public professional activity that demonstrates expertise beyond the therapy room. Even a single published article in a local newspaper about managing anxiety builds an external credibility signal.

For solo practices, the therapist profile and the about page are functionally the same thing. Invest in it accordingly. The [American Psychological Association's guidance on therapist digital presence](https://www.apa.org/) is a useful north star for what constitutes verifiable professional credibility online.

## Pillar 4: Schema markup for mental health practices

Structured data is how AI engines and search platforms understand, without ambiguity, what type of healthcare provider you are, what you treat, and where you practice. Most therapy websites have no schema at all.

**For the practice itself:**

```json
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "MedicalBusiness",
  "additionalType": "https://schema.org/MentalHealthCare",
  "name": "Westside Therapy Center",
  "url": "https://westsidetherapy.com",
  "telephone": "+1-555-000-0000",
  "address": {
    "@type": "PostalAddress",
    "streetAddress": "123 Oak Street, Suite 200",
    "addressLocality": "Portland",
    "addressRegion": "OR",
    "postalCode": "97201",
    "addressCountry": "US"
  },
  "medicalSpecialty": "Psychiatry",
  "availableService": [
    {
      "@type": "MedicalTherapy",
      "name": "Cognitive Behavioral Therapy"
    },
    {
      "@type": "MedicalTherapy",
      "name": "EMDR Therapy"
    }
  ],
  "hasMap": "https://maps.google.com/...",
  "openingHoursSpecification": [...]
}
```

**For individual therapists:**

```json
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Psychologist",
  "name": "Dr. Sarah Chen, PsyD",
  "url": "https://westsidetherapy.com/team/sarah-chen",
  "hasCredential": [
    {
      "@type": "EducationalOccupationalCredential",
      "credentialCategory": "Doctoral Degree",
      "recognizedBy": {
        "@type": "Organization",
        "name": "Pacific University"
      }
    },
    {
      "@type": "EducationalOccupationalCredential",
      "credentialCategory": "State License",
      "name": "Oregon Licensed Psychologist"
    }
  ],
  "worksFor": {
    "@type": "MedicalBusiness",
    "name": "Westside Therapy Center"
  },
  "knowsAbout": ["PTSD", "Trauma", "EMDR", "Cognitive Behavioral Therapy"]
}
```

Use `Psychologist` type for licensed psychologists (PhD, PsyD), `MentalHealthCare` as the `additionalType` for the practice, and `Person` schema with `hasCredential` for counselors and therapists. The `knowsAbout` property on individual therapist schema provides AI engines with the list of conditions and modalities to match against queries. For the full schema implementation framework, see our [schema markup guide](/seo/schema-markup/).

## E-E-A-T for mental health: what the signals actually are

Google's Quality Rater Guidelines call out mental health content explicitly as requiring the highest standards of expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness. In practice, this means:

**License first.** The licensure type and number should appear on the first screen of every page authored by or about a therapist. "Dr. Jones is a Licensed Professional Counselor (LPC, License #OR12345)" is not bureaucratic, it's a trust signal that AI engines can verify against state licensing boards.

**Training specificity matters more than volume.** "Trained in trauma-focused approaches" signals nothing. "Completed Level 1 and Level 2 EMDR training through EMDRIA-approved provider, 2021" signals something an AI engine can cross-reference.

**Named authorship on all clinical content.** Specialty and modality pages should carry a byline, even if the therapist did not write every word, they should review and sign off. "Written by Sarah Chen, PsyD. Reviewed June 2026." Both authorship and recency matter for YMYL pages.

**Third-party professional footprint.** Psychology Today therapist profiles, Zencare listings, Headway profiles, and similar directories create the external corroboration that makes your own website's claims verifiable. These also drive direct traffic and rank in their own right.

**Community presence.** Teaching a workshop, contributing to a mental health awareness campaign, writing a guest post for a local publication, any public professional activity that creates an indexed, dated record of expertise contributes to E-E-A-T. For a deeper treatment of the framework, see our [E-E-A-T guide](/seo/eeat/).

## A sensitive note on reviews

Reviews are important for local search. A Google Business Profile for a therapy practice with 50 reviews and a 4.8 average will outrank a competitor with 8 reviews across most local pack queries. The [local SEO checklist](/blog/local-seo-checklist-2026/) covers the mechanics.

The ethics and legal constraints around therapy practice reviews require care:

**HIPAA considerations.** Responding to a Google review in a way that confirms someone is a patient is a potential HIPAA violation. Many therapists choose not to respond to reviews at all, or respond only in general terms that neither confirm nor deny the reviewer's status. The [HHS guidance on HIPAA and online reviews](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/index.html) is the authoritative source for what is and isn't permissible.

**Ethical boundaries.** The therapeutic relationship involves a power differential and genuine trust. Asking clients directly for reviews, especially inside the therapy relationship, introduces a dynamic most professional ethics codes caution against. The appropriate approach is a general, opt-in note: something on your post-session materials or client portal indicating that reviews are appreciated if clients feel comfortable leaving one, without any direct request in session.

**What to do instead of aggressive review solicitation.** Build your GBP with rich practice information, service categories, and regular photo updates. Ensure every directory profile (Psychology Today, Zencare, Headway, TherapyDen) is complete and current. Encourage former clients, once the therapeutic relationship has formally ended, through passive channels like a follow-up email. Volume will accumulate more slowly than a dental practice, and that is appropriate given the context.

## Comparing your digital presence to what AI sees

Most therapists are aware of Psychology Today's therapist directory. It ranks well, drives referrals, and the profile structure forces you to list specialties, insurance, and fees. This is not a substitute for your own website's authority, it's a benchmark for what structured, complete information looks like from an AI engine's perspective.

If a Psychology Today profile can rank on page one for "[specialty] therapist [city]" queries with templated content, your own website, with deeper clinical content, credential schema, and original modality pages, can do the same and more. The directory wins because it has structured data and specificity. Replicate that structure on a domain you own.

## FAQ

**How long does it take for therapy practice SEO to produce results?**

For local pack rankings, a fully optimized Google Business Profile with consistent citations and growing review velocity typically produces measurable movement in 60 to 90 days. For AI Overview citations and broader content rankings, expect 3 to 6 months for new specialty pages to gain traction. The investment compounds: practices that build specialty page infrastructure in the first half of 2026 will be the default cited sources by late 2026 and into 2027.

**Should solo practitioners invest in SEO, or is Psychology Today enough?**

Psychology Today is useful for directory presence and is worth maintaining. It is not a substitute for a website you control. Directories can change algorithms, raise prices, or lose prominence. Your own website, once it has established authority, is a durable asset. Solo practitioners with a strong specialty focus, EMDR for first responders, DBT for adolescents, therapy for LGBTQ+ adults, can rank very well with targeted content and a modest budget.

**What is the most important first step for a new therapy practice website?**

Therapist profile page with full credentials, licensure, and clinical approach, then one deep specialty page for your primary focus area, followed by the insurance and fees page. In that order. These three pages, done well, do more than a 20-page generic website.

**How should a group practice handle SEO differently than a solo practice?**

Group practices should treat each therapist as a content pillar. Individual therapist profile pages with schema, specialty pages attributed to specific clinicians, and GBP optimization per location (if you have multiple offices) compound across a larger content footprint. The group's homepage should position the practice's overall specialties; individual therapist pages should do the specificity work.

**Can telehealth-only practices compete in local SEO?**

Yes, with a state-based strategy. A therapist licensed in California, Oregon, and Washington can target local queries in each state with state-specific landing pages. GBP is harder without a physical address, but practice SEO and AI Overview citations from content do not require a local map pack presence. Telehealth practices should explicitly list every licensed state on the website and in schema.

**How do AI engines handle sensitive mental health queries?**

AI engines apply conservative citation standards to sensitive mental health queries, particularly crisis-related searches. Practices that build deep, medically accurate content on conditions like PTSD, OCD, and eating disorders, authored by credentialed clinicians, do get cited for treatment and therapy-seeking queries. Crisis queries ("I'm having thoughts of suicide") typically route to crisis resources rather than practice recommendations. Ranking for treatment queries, "PTSD therapist [city]," "OCD specialist [city]", is the right goal, not crisis intervention content.
