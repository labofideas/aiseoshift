The best AI agents for insurance in 2027 are Verisk, Hi Marley and Sixfold, followed by Sapiens, Pibit.ai, Majesco, CCC, Kalepa, Indico Data and Charlee.ai. We ranked them on documented oversight, defined metrics, regulatory readiness, evidence and scope. This is an editorial comparison, not insurance, legal or financial advice.

## Key takeaways

- Insurers say they use AI nearly everywhere. NAIC surveys found 88 percent of auto, 70 percent of home, 58 percent of life and 92 percent of health insurers use, plan to use or plan to explore it.
- The regulator's question is accountability. The NAIC model bulletin expects a written AI program and audit rights over the vendors whose models you buy.
- No vendor on this list publishes a price, so price cannot separate them.
- Metrics are mostly undefined. Pibit.ai publishes five headline numbers with no definitions, and Kalepa's "960BP" has no stated baseline.
- Vendor risk is real. Indico Data disclosed a May 2026 incident that may have exposed Social Security numbers held for corporate customers.

## What changed in 2026

A year ago the insurance AI question was whether to pilot. In 2026 it became who answers when the pilot decides something about a customer.

Three things moved. State adoption of the NAIC model bulletin kept spreading: Mississippi, Puerto Rico, South Carolina and North Dakota all carry 2026 adoption dates on a map updated November 3, 2026. Regulators started building a tool to examine AI systems, which the NAIC says was in a 12-state pilot as of March 2026 and is expected to come up at the 2026 Fall National Meeting. And the money got large enough for public filings. CCC reported AI-related revenue of about $130 million annualized in the third quarter of 2026, up 40 percent, in an [October 29 release](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001818201/000119312526436974/ccc-ex99_1.htm).

Consumers also began to arrive through AI. J.D. Power's first U.S. AI Insurance Experience Study, reported September 1, 2026, found 29 percent of auto and home customers had used AI for research, servicing, claims understanding or shopping.

## What counts as an AI agent in insurance

The label is stretched. Four things get called agents: a **reader** that pulls loss runs, forms or estimates into fields, a **scorer** that rates a risk or a claim, a **messenger** that talks to a policyholder, and an **actor** that routes, pays, issues or binds. Only the actor changes a record someone else relies on, and few vendors here say they let an agent act without a person. For the generic definition, see our guide to [AI agents for business](/blog/ai-agents-for-business/).

## The market in numbers

Every figure below was read on its source page in November 2026.

**Adoption.** The NAIC's [AI topic page](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/artificial-intelligence) summarizes four surveys. In auto, 88 percent of 193 responding insurers said they use, plan to use or plan to explore AI and machine learning (December 2022). Home was 70 percent of 194 (August 2023), life 58 percent of 161 (December 2023) and health 92 percent of 93 (May 2025). "Plan to explore" is not "use," and the years differ, so do not stack these into one adoption rate.

**Who builds the models.** Roughly half of the models insurers use for marketing were built by third-party vendors, while auto and home insurers mostly built pricing and underwriting models in-house. That is why the NAIC has a working group on third-party data and models.

**Consumer use.** J.D. Power's 2026 U.S. AI Insurance Experience Study, fielded in June and July 2026 across 8,352 customer evaluations of 24 brands and eight third-party AI tools, found 25 percent of auto and home customers used insurer tools to get quotes and 21 percent used third-party solutions. Of those who used AI to shop for a new policy, 42 percent bought one as a result. Customers were least comfortable using AI to change policies, process claims or handle customer service. We read a [CarPro write-up](https://www.carpro.com/blog/j.d.-power-more-people-are-using-ai-to-shop-for-insurance), not the J.D. Power release.

**Cancellations.** Gartner predicted in June 2025 that [over 40 percent of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027) because of cost, unclear value or inadequate risk controls. The poll had 3,412 webinar attendees across all industries.

**Litigation.** In *Estate of Lokken v. UnitedHealth Group*, family members of two deceased Medicare Advantage beneficiaries allege UnitedHealthcare used a predictive tool, nH Predict, to override physicians and deny care. A law-firm [alert](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/federal-court-orders-broad-discovery-against-uhc-ai-coverage-denial-lawsuit) says a magistrate judge's March 9, 2026 order largely granted a motion to compel, including documents on the insurer's internal AI review board. Contract claims survived an earlier motion and others were dismissed on preemption grounds. Optum disputes the allegations. This is discovery, not a merits ruling.

The pattern: intent is high, consumers are starting to use AI, and the evidence about agents specifically is mostly vendor-supplied.

## How we ranked them

This ranking uses vendor pages, filings and press releases opened in November 2026. We did not test any product, and no vendor gave us anything. It scores what a buyer can check, not how well each product works. Each product scores 1 to 5 on five criteria, for a maximum of 25.

| Criterion | What earns a 5 | What earns a 1 |
|---|---|---|
| Oversight | Documented audit log, version history, approval gates or undo | "Humans stay in control" and nothing else |
| Metric definitions | Headline numbers defined, with baseline and conditions | Percentages with no definition |
| Regulatory readiness | Names the rules it supports and documents how | No mention of insurance regulation or third-party audit |
| Evidence | Named customers with checkable results, or audited financials | Logos and unattributed testimonials |
| Scope clarity | A clear statement of what it does and does not do | Broad "autonomous insurance" claims |

Ties break on oversight, then definitions. Price is out of the scoring because no vendor publishes one. Where a vendor makes no numeric claims, we score definitions in the middle, since silence cannot mislead. Nobody tops 18, and that is the finding.

Three disclosures. Anthropic's Claude helped draft this article. Verisk, our top-ranked vendor, announced an integration with Claude in May 2026, so weigh our ranking of Verisk accordingly. And we have no commercial relationship with any vendor listed.

We chose ten, not the whole market. Several large insurance AI vendors are covered in sibling rankings on our other sites and are left out here. We dropped Gradient AI because its public page offers little to score beyond deployment counts and SOC 2 and HITRUST claims. Flyreel's old address now redirects to a LexisNexis Risk Solutions page, and Planck says it is now part of a larger agency-software company, so neither counts as a standalone pick.

## The 10 agents, ranked

### 1. Verisk

**Score: 18 of 25** (oversight 4, definitions 2, regulatory readiness 4, evidence 4, scope 4).

Verisk's [May 5, 2026 release](https://s29.q4cdn.com/767340216/files/doc_news/Verisk-Brings-Its-Trusted-Analytics-and-Generative-AI-Capabilities-Directly-into-Anthropics-Claude-2026.pdf) announced two Model Context Protocol connectors that put its data inside Claude. Verisk Underwriting Intelligence gives underwriters and actuaries conversational access to ISO loss cost trends, experience insights and filing signals. XactRestore does the same for pricing and estimating intelligence from Xactware. The release says Verisk has deployed approximately 40 agentic and generative AI solutions and that its analytics are used by the top 100 U.S. property and casualty insurers.

Who it suits: carriers and actuarial teams that already license Verisk data, and contractors on Xactware. Pricing: none published.

Oversight is stated as policy. Work happens inside "established, controlled workflows," data access follows Verisk's governance framework, and "accountability remains with people." No audit log or approval gate is described, hence a 4. The time savings, "hundreds of hours per carrier per year" and 30 minutes to two hours per estimate, are both labeled estimates with no method.

Real limits. These are connectors to a data product, not an autonomous underwriter, and the verisk.com pages we opened returned only titles, so the release is our main source.

Use case: an underwriter asks for loss cost trends on a class while preparing an indication, then checks the answer against filing signals before quoting.

Verdict: the best-documented position in the field, partly because it describes a narrow integration. It is also the least "agent" of the ten.

### 2. Hi Marley

**Score: 17 of 25** (oversight 5, definitions 2, regulatory readiness 3, evidence 4, scope 3).

[Hi Marley](https://www.himarley.com) is a messaging layer for property and casualty claims and servicing. Products include Conversational FNOL, Workflow Assist, Total Loss Assist and Claims Inquiry Assist, and its AI recommends next steps and runs steps such as repair evaluation, rental selection and appointment reminders. It also shows proactive renewal and autopay notices. The company says it has more than 130 customers, including 13 of the top 30 P&C carriers.

Who it suits: carriers that lose time to phone tag in claims. Pricing: not published.

Its [security and compliance page](https://www.himarley.com/security-and-compliance) has the most concrete oversight we found: a Case Audit Log for oversight and dispute resolution, transcripts sent to the carrier's system of record, role-based permissions, and AI that is "observable and auditable" with changes "versioned, approved, monitored, and revertible."

Metrics are the weak spot: 35 to 50 percent inbound call reduction, 60 to 90 percent voicemail reduction and 30 percent cycle time reduction, with no baselines. Case studies attach single numbers to carriers, such as Encova's 46 percent cycle time reduction.

Real limits. That page does not mention the Telephone Consumer Protection Act or double opt-in, and lists SOC 2 Type II without a date. Ask for the consent workflow and the report.

Use case: a policyholder reports a loss by text, uploads photos, and the adjuster sees the transcript in the claim file.

Verdict: the strongest audit story for a customer-facing agent. Scope scores a 3 because "AI and automation" covers a lot of ground.

### 3. Sixfold

**Score: 17 of 25** (oversight 4, definitions 2, regulatory readiness 3, evidence 4, scope 4).

[Sixfold](https://www.sixfold.ai) sells an underwriting agent for two markets. For life and health, it reviews prescription histories, lab results and driving records and surfaces impairments with a suggested next step. For commercial property and casualty, an "AI Underwriter" reads loss runs and schedules of values, scores risk and recommends actions. Named customers include Mosaic, Skyward Specialty, Guardian, Zurich North America, AXIS and Generali GC&C.

Who it suits: commercial underwriters with submission backlogs and life and health carriers with case queues. Pricing: not published.

Life and health is described as "governed AI, underwriters review and own the final call." Property and casualty scores risk "with explainable signals, every fact cited to source." The page does not mention audit logs. It links a Responsible AI Report with five pillars, from human oversight to data governance, which we did not open.

Metrics: 50 percent improved efficiency, 15 percent increased quote-to-bind ratio, 30 percent more gross written premium per underwriter. No baseline, period or method. A Guardian executive's 50 percent review-time claim is the customer's statement.

Real limits. Source citation helps an underwriter check a fact. It does not tell a regulator how the score was built, so ask for model documentation your AI program can file.

Use case: a life underwriter reads the surfaced impairments and cited records, then decides.

Verdict: a credible human-in-the-loop agent with real customers. The numbers are slogans until Sixfold defines them.

### 4. Sapiens

**Score: 16 of 25** (oversight 4, definitions 3, regulatory readiness 3, evidence 2, scope 4).

Sapiens calls SapiensAIP "The Autonomous Insurance Platform." Its [product page](https://www.sapiens.com/solutions/sapiensaip/) describes a decision-intelligence "Brain," "Agents" for underwriting, claims and servicing built in Agent Studio, and "Surfaces" for customers, brokers, agents and employers. Lines listed are life, property and casualty, reinsurance, global specialty and workers' compensation.

Who it suits: carriers already on Sapiens Core. The page says SapiensAIP supports Core and up to two prior versions, not third-party cores, which is the clearest scope statement in this group and also a limit. Pricing: not published.

The page claims audit trails for AI decisions and actions, explainability, configurable guardrails, human-in-the-loop oversight and design with "emerging AI regulation and insurance-sector requirements in mind." It does not say how any of that works.

Metrics: none for the AI, so we score definitions in the middle. The main site cites 600-plus customers and a survey of 475 senior insurance executives, neither defined.

Real limits. Little public evidence of what the agents do in production.

Use case: a carrier on Core builds a claims intake agent, sets guardrails, and routes exceptions to adjusters.

Verdict: the right kind of answer for policy administration shops, and frank about compatibility. Evidence scores a 2 because the page proves little.

### 5. Pibit.ai

**Score: 16 of 25** (oversight 4, definitions 1, regulatory readiness 3, evidence 4, scope 4).

[Pibit.ai](https://www.pibit.ai) sells an "AI underwriting OS" with six modules: intake and clearance, extraction and research, triage and risk analysis, rating and pricing, quote and bind, and portfolio management. It handles loss runs, ACORDs, motor vehicle records, schedules of values and experience mods across "10+ LOBs." Case studies include CAMICO, Foresight, Kinetic, HDVI and Shepherd.

Who it suits: commercial carriers and MGAs that want a submission-to-bind workbench. Pricing: not published.

Oversight features are specific: "an explainable risk score for every submission built from individually scored factors," a link from every number back to its origin page, version history that keeps every version and decision with the submission, and ISO 27001 and SOC 2 Type II badges.

Metrics are the problem. The headline says hit ratio up 20 percent, throughput 2x and quote time down 70 percent. Case studies add 190 percent more submissions per underwriter, a 96 percent cut in loss run analysis time and 99.9 percent coverage and accuracy. The page defines none of these. Hit ratio is the one that touches an underwriting P&L, so ask about it first.

Real limits. No bias testing, model governance or override process is described.

Use case: a workers' compensation underwriter sees fields traced to source pages and checks factor scores before quoting.

Verdict: strong traceability, weak numbers. Demo the source tracing.

### 6. Majesco

**Score: 14 of 25** (oversight 3, definitions 3, regulatory readiness 2, evidence 2, scope 4).

[Majesco](https://www.majesco.com) says its platform "brings governed AI agents into P&C, LA&H and Retirement and Pension workflows." It lists claims triage and assignment, an agentic payment resolver and benefit determination, with policy, billing and distribution management in the core suite. The Fall '26 release is described as AI agents working alongside people in a shared case environment.

Who it suits: carriers replacing or extending core systems that want agents on the same platform. The site claims 375-plus customers and 1,400-plus implementations, neither defined. Pricing: not published.

The metrics are at least honest about being estimates. Triage and assignment drops from 70 minutes to 2, suspended payment resolution from 10 minutes to 30 seconds, benefit determination from 45 minutes to about 4.5, all labeled "modeled benchmarks." No baseline or method.

Oversight is the word "governed" and a trust center link. No audit trail or approval gate detail.

Real limits. Modeled benchmarks are not production results, and the Spring and Fall release press pages would not open for us.

Use case: a payment suspended on a missing field is flagged, the agent proposes a fix, and a processor approves it.

Verdict: the "modeled benchmark" label is the right instinct. Pair it with a pilot on your own data.

**Score: 14 of 25** (oversight 2, definitions 2, regulatory readiness 2, evidence 5, scope 3).

[CCC](https://www.cccis.com) is the claims network behind many auto physical damage workflows, with carrier solutions for claims management, casualty, fraud detection and subrogation. Its site claims 17 million-plus claims processed annually and 800 million vehicle damage photos, and counts 300-plus auto insurers.

Who it suits: auto insurers, repairers and parts suppliers already on the network. Pricing: not published.

Evidence is the strength, because it is a public company. The [Q3 release](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001818201/000119312526436974/ccc-ex99_1.htm) puts AI-related revenue at about $130 million annualized, up 40 percent, from about $120 million in the second quarter. An [October 15, 2026 release](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001818201/000119312526422320/d140608dex991.htm) says CCC agreed to be acquired by GTCR and Elliott at an enterprise value of about $6.1 billion, with closing expected in the first quarter of 2027. Its chief executive said the new owners would help it "accelerate the AI and agentic capabilities our customers want from us."

Oversight on the pages we opened is thin: "Guided by your processes and controls." The page's structured data also contains obvious errors, such as an aggregate rating built from the claims-processed count.

Real limits. Run-rate revenue shows spending, not outcomes.

Use case: a carrier routes an auto claim with an AI total-loss flag, and an adjuster reviews the estimate.

Verdict: the best financial evidence on the list and the least public detail on controls. A pending ownership change belongs in any multi-year contract talk.

### 8. Kalepa

**Score: 13 of 25** (oversight 2, definitions 1, regulatory readiness 2, evidence 4, scope 4).

[Kalepa](https://www.kalepa.com) sells underwriting software across seven modules: submission ingestion, clearance, triage, risk analysis, rating, quote and bind, and portfolio management. It targets carriers, MGAs, mutuals and brokers. The logo wall includes Paragon, Berkley, Canopius, Munich Re, Church Mutual and Bowhead.

Who it suits: commercial underwriting shops that want an end-to-end workbench. Pricing: not published.

Metrics: "960BP combined ratio improvement," "30%+ more premium per underwriter" and "58% reduction in quote time." The page defines no baseline, period or even the abbreviation. A combined ratio improvement is the most consequential claim an underwriting vendor can make, and it arrives without context.

Oversight: an "AI Ensemble Engine" selects a model or method in real time and decides when to automate and when to amplify underwriter judgment. The page does not say how, or whether that choice is logged.

Real limits. The NAIC bulletin expects documentation of how AI systems produce outcomes, and "proprietary ensemble" is not documentation.

Use case: a submission arrives by email, is triaged and rated, and an underwriter reviews it before quoting.

Verdict: wide product, many customers, almost no definitions. Ask what 960BP is measured against.

### 9. Indico Data

**Score: 12 of 25** (oversight 3, definitions 1, regulatory readiness 1, evidence 3, scope 4).

[Indico Data](https://www.indicodata.ai) automates intake for commercial underwriting. It ingests emails, documents, spreadsheets, loss runs and schedules of values and screens submissions against carrier appetite and guidelines. Logos shown include Allstate, Sompo, Markel and Aviva, but the page does not label them customers.

Who it suits: commercial carriers drowning in submissions. Pricing: not published.

Metrics: 85 percent submission processing efficiency, $30 million in added premium, under 30 seconds per schedule or loss run and 40 percent lower processing costs. No baseline or method. It promises "the controls, validation, and human oversight required for production" and "the rationale behind each result."

Regulatory readiness scores a 1 because of an incident. It says it rotated credentials, added monitoring and tightened access controls. The notice is hosted on a law-firm site, not Indico's own.

Real limits. The NAIC bulletin expects insurers to keep due diligence and audit rights over vendors, and a recent incident is a diligence question, not a disqualifier.

Use case: a broker email arrives, Indico extracts the schedule, and an underwriter sees an appetite fit before reading a page.

Verdict: a useful intake product with a disclosed incident. Put it in the diligence file.

### 10. Charlee.ai

**Score: 10 of 25** (oversight 2, definitions 1, regulatory readiness 1, evidence 2, scope 4).

[Charlee.ai](https://www.charlee.ai) sells claims intelligence for property and casualty carriers. 4SeeCharlee forecasts attorney involvement, litigation, severity and reserves. DocuCharlee summarizes claim files. AskCharlee queries claims data. FindCharlee flags suspicious claims. RiteCharlee supports underwriting and risk selection. Listed technology partners include Guidewire, Duck Creek, Majesco, OneShield and Sapiens.

In our reading, that absence matters most here, because litigation prediction and fraud flags are the claims uses closest to what is alleged in the UnitedHealth case. Anything scoring claims for fraud or litigation belongs in your AI inventory with a named owner.

Use case: a claim is scored for litigation risk early, and a senior adjuster reviews the flag before handling changes.

Verdict: a clear product map with the least public governance detail on the list. Ask for model documentation before the demo.

## How insurance agents change who gets found and cited

Insurance is a comparison-shopping category, and shoppers are starting to compare through AI.

J.D. Power found 29 percent of auto and home customers used AI for research, servicing, claims understanding or shopping, and 42 percent of those who used it to shop bought a policy. It also found customers least comfortable letting AI change policies or process claims. People will let an assistant help them compare. They are not yet comfortable letting it act. We found no study of how often assistants name a specific carrier or agency, and we are not going to invent one.

The agents in this article work on the other side of that conversation: quoting, first notice of loss and servicing run through carrier and vendor systems. What a shopper or assistant can read is the public layer of rate filings, coverage explainers, claims process pages, agent bios, reviews and structured data. Our inference, labeled as such: carriers and agencies that publish plain, current, machine-readable facts will be quoted more accurately, and the rest will be described from whatever else is on the web.

## How to choose

Start from the decision you need to defend, not the feature you want.

**Commercial underwriting.** Compare Sixfold, Pibit.ai, Kalepa and Indico Data on one question: can an underwriter see where each number came from, and does the system keep it? Pibit.ai and Sixfold document sourcing most clearly.

**Life and health underwriting.** Sixfold is the only one here with a dedicated life and health agent. Annex III treats pricing of natural persons in life and health as high-risk, so ask for documentation that fits.

**Claims.** Hi Marley covers the policyholder conversation, CCC the auto network, Charlee.ai litigation and fraud signals. Treat the scoring tools as the higher-risk category.

**Policy administration.** Start with your core vendor. If it is Sapiens or Majesco, ask for the agent governance documents, because neither publishes detail.

**Data and actuarial support.** Verisk's connectors sit on data you already license. Note the Claude connection we disclosed.

## Implementation, governance and risk

**Know what the NAIC model bulletin asks for.** The [model bulletin](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/inline-files/2023-12-4%20Model%20Bulletin_Adopted_0.pdf) was adopted December 4, 2023. It says decisions made or supported by AI systems must comply with insurance law, including unfair trade practices and unfair discrimination. It expects a written AIS Program with governance, risk controls and internal audit under senior management. For third parties it points to due diligence and, where appropriate, contract terms giving audit rights and requiring cooperation with regulators.

**Know whether your state adopted it.** The NAIC's [implementation map](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/legal-adoption-map-ai-model-bulletin.pdf), dated November 3, 2026, lists 29 adopted entries: 27 states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico. California, Colorado, New York and Texas appear separately under insurance-specific regulation or guidance. The map says it does not judge whether a state's version contains every element, so read your state's text.

**Know Colorado.** The Colorado Division of Insurance's [August 2025 notice](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/CODORA/bulletins/3ee9c3f) says an amended regulation took effect October 15, 2025. It covers insurers offering individual life, private passenger auto or health benefit plans and sets governance and risk management requirements for external consumer data and for algorithms and models that use it. The NAIC map cites it as 3 CCR 702-10. It is commonly associated with Colorado's SB21-169, but we did not open the statute and describe only the regulation.

**Know the EU line.** Annex III of the EU AI Act, per an [unofficial annotated copy](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/), lists "risk assessment and pricing in relation to natural persons in the case of life and health insurance" as high-risk. It concerns natural persons, so commercial property underwriting is a different question. We did not verify application dates and state none.

**Treat the vendor as part of your control environment.** The Indico incident is the reminder. Put audit rights, breach notice terms and model documentation in the contract.

**Watch the courts.** The UnitedHealth case is a Medicare Advantage health case about one tool, so it sets no rule for other lines. It does show what plaintiffs ask for in discovery: the internal AI review board and the business case behind the tool. If you cannot produce your own versions, that is the gap.

**Measure on your own data.** Run any agent in suggestion mode for a month and count wrong extractions, wrong scores and overridden recommendations before it acts. For anything that changes a coverage position or pays or denies a claim, keep a named human in the path until your error rate is one you would show a regulator.

None of this is insurance, legal or financial advice. Have your counsel and compliance team review your own situation.

## 2027 outlook

Expect three things. Regulators will ask vendors for documentation more directly, because the NAIC is building tools for it. Procurement will ask for defined metrics, since even Majesco's "modeled" label beats a bare percentage. And consolidation will continue: CCC's pending ownership change is one example, and Gartner estimates only about 130 of the thousands of agentic vendors are real.

The consumer side will likely move faster than the carrier side. Shoppers already let AI help them compare, and carriers with clean, readable public facts will be quoted more accurately when assistants can start a quote.

The sharp line: in insurance, "the AI decided" is not an answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the best AI agents for insurance in 2027?

On our oversight-first rubric, Verisk scores highest at 18 of 25, followed by Hi Marley, Sixfold, Sapiens, Pibit.ai, Majesco, CCC, Kalepa, Indico Data and Charlee.ai. The right choice depends on whether you need underwriting, claims or policy servicing, and on how much documentation your regulator expects.

### Are AI agents allowed to make underwriting or claims decisions?

Regulators do not ban it, but they hold the insurer responsible. The NAIC model bulletin says decisions made or supported by AI systems must comply with insurance law, including unfair trade practices and unfair discrimination, whatever tools are used. Most vendors on this list say a human keeps the final call. This is not insurance, legal or financial advice.

### How many states have adopted the NAIC AI model bulletin?

The NAIC's implementation map, dated November 3, 2026, lists 29 adopted entries: 27 states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico. California, Colorado, New York and Texas are listed separately as having their own insurance-specific rules or guidance.

### Does Colorado regulate insurer AI?

Yes. The Colorado Division of Insurance adopted an amended regulation effective October 15, 2025 that sets governance and risk management requirements for insurers offering individual life, private passenger auto or health benefit plans when they use external consumer data and the algorithms and models built on it.

### Does the EU AI Act cover insurance?

Annex III of the EU AI Act, in an unofficial annotated copy we opened, lists AI used for risk assessment and pricing of natural persons in life and health insurance among high-risk uses. We did not verify application dates, so we state none. Check with counsel.

### Can an AI agent be sued over a claim denial?

The insurer is the defendant, not the software. In Estate of Lokken v. UnitedHealth Group, plaintiffs allege the insurer used a predictive tool to override physicians. A federal court let breach of contract claims proceed and ordered broad discovery in March 2026. Optum disputes the allegations, and there is no merits ruling.
