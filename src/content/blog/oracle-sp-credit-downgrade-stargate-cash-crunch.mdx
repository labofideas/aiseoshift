On July 9, 2026, S&P Global Ratings cut Oracle's long-term credit rating from BBB to BBB-, one notch above junk, and dropped its short-term rating from A-2 to A-3. The outlook is stable, so this isn't a company in crisis. It is a company whose bond rating now reflects just how much of its future depends on one customer, and how much cash it is burning to serve that customer.

The customer is OpenAI. S&P's note named OpenAI concentration risk directly, pointing out that OpenAI accounts for nearly half of Oracle's $638 billion order backlog. The agency also forecast a free operating cash flow deficit of almost $42 billion for Oracle's 2027 fiscal year. That's the number that should stop anyone reading this and thinking it's routine.

## TL;DR

S&P downgraded Oracle's credit rating to BBB- on July 9, 2026, citing extreme customer concentration (OpenAI represents close to half of Oracle's $638 billion backlog) and a projected $42 billion free-cash-flow deficit in fiscal 2027. The downgrade is the clearest signal yet of the price Oracle is paying to fund its role in Stargate, the roughly $500 billion AI data center project it shares with OpenAI, SoftBank, and MGX, on top of a separate five-year, roughly $300 billion cloud contract with OpenAI signed in September 2025. Oracle had already cut its workforce by a confirmed net 21,000 people (of up to 30,000 gross terminations) back in March 2026 to help fund the buildout, and by July that cost discipline wasn't enough to stop credit default swaps on Oracle debt from climbing to an 18-year high and a Wisconsin utility dispute from demanding more than $7 billion in collateral for a single data center. Oracle's stock fell from roughly $149 in early July to around $115 by month's end.

## What S&P actually said

The downgrade rests on two linked concerns, both spelled out in the rating action:

- **Concentration risk.** OpenAI alone makes up close to half of Oracle's $638 billion remaining performance obligations, the accounting measure of contracted future revenue. A single customer at that scale, however creditworthy, is a structural risk rating agencies price in.
- **Cash burn.** S&P forecasts Oracle running a free operating cash flow deficit of almost $42 billion in fiscal 2027, as capital expenditure on data centers and GPUs keeps outrunning the revenue those assets generate.

BBB- is still investment grade. It's the lowest rung of investment grade, one step above speculative (junk) status, which is why the market treated this as a real signal rather than noise. Five-year credit default swaps on Oracle's debt climbed to their highest level in roughly 18 years in the days around the downgrade.

## Why Oracle is stretched this thin

None of this happened in isolation. Oracle spent the first half of 2026 trading headcount for GPU capacity, and the downgrade is the balance-sheet consequence of that trade becoming visible to credit markets.

Oracle confirmed a net workforce reduction of 21,000 people, from 162,000 to 141,000, in its fiscal 2026 annual report filed with the SEC in June, the tail end of cuts that some reporting put as high as 30,000 gross terminations executed on March 31. Oracle Health and its SaaS/Virtual Operations Services unit each lost roughly 30% of headcount, while Oracle Cloud Infrastructure was largely spared and kept hiring. Executive Vice Chair Safra Catz described the move as "a generational reallocation of capital from people-intensive consulting and legacy support toward GPU-intensive AI infrastructure."

That reallocation is funding Oracle's side of Stargate, the roughly $500 billion AI infrastructure project announced at the White House in January 2025 with OpenAI, SoftBank, and the Abu Dhabi vehicle MGX. Oracle's exposure inside that structure got substantially bigger in September 2025, when it signed a five-year cloud computing contract with OpenAI worth a reported $300 billion, under which Oracle is expected to supply roughly 4.5 gigawatts of compute, about what four million US homes draw in electricity.

The capital numbers explain the strain. Oracle guided to roughly $50 billion in capital expenditure for fiscal 2026, up 3.5x from fiscal 2024's $14.2 billion, with actual capex reportedly landing closer to $55.7 billion. Free cash flow for the year came in around negative $23.7 billion. TD Cowen estimated the March layoffs alone would free up $8 billion to $10 billion in annual cash flow. It wasn't enough to hold the rating.

## The collateral problem the downgrade created

A credit downgrade isn't just a headline. It has mechanical consequences, and Wisconsin is the clearest example. Because Oracle's credit rating slipped, the state's largest utility can now require Oracle to post more than $7 billion in cash or a letter of credit to cover a planned Port Washington data center, adding an estimated $100 million a year in financing cost just to keep that one project moving. That's a direct, contractual trigger tied to the rating, not investor sentiment.

Oracle's stock had actually rallied 2% to 6% when the March layoffs were executed, on the theory that Wall Street wanted the cost discipline. That goodwill didn't survive the summer. Shares fell from roughly $149 in early July to around $115 by month's end, as the downgrade, the Wisconsin dispute, and broader tech-sector wobbles compounded.

## Why it matters beyond Oracle

Oracle isn't the only company running this trade. Microsoft cut roughly 4,800 jobs in July 2026 even as it kept expanding AI capex. Amazon and Meta have run comparable plays through the year: shrink headcount in mature, people-heavy businesses, and redirect the savings and new debt into GPUs and data centers.

What's different about Oracle is that a rating agency just put a number on the risk. Remaining performance obligations at $638 billion, up sharply year over year, is the bull case for why any of this is rational: Oracle is pre-selling years of compute to committed customers. The bear case is that when the single largest customer inside that backlog is a company still burning cash itself, the credit risk doesn't stay theoretical. It shows up in a downgrade, then in a collateral demand, then in a stock price.

July 9, 2026. S&P cut Oracle's long-term issuer credit rating from BBB to BBB-, and its short-term rating from A-2 to A-3, with a stable outlook. BBB- is the lowest investment-grade tier, one notch above junk.

**Why did S&P downgrade Oracle?**
Two reasons: extreme customer concentration, since OpenAI represents nearly half of Oracle's $638 billion order backlog, and heavy cash burn, with S&P forecasting a free operating cash flow deficit of almost $42 billion for fiscal 2027 as AI infrastructure capex keeps outpacing revenue.

**How does this connect to Oracle's layoffs?**
Oracle confirmed a net 21,000-person workforce reduction (of up to 30,000 gross terminations) executed on March 31, 2026, explicitly to help fund its AI infrastructure buildout. The July downgrade shows that cost-cutting wasn't enough on its own to hold Oracle's prior credit rating given the scale of its Stargate and OpenAI commitments.

Stargate is a roughly $500 billion AI data center project announced in January 2025 by OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle, and the Abu Dhabi investment vehicle MGX.
