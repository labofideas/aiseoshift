Transcription used to be a chore you outsourced or avoided. In 2026 it is a background utility that runs while you work, and the quality is good enough that most teams no longer think about it until it gets something wrong.

The category splits into two jobs that look similar but solve different problems. Live meeting assistants sit inside your calls, capture what was said in real time, and hand back notes, summaries, and action items. File-based transcription takes a finished recording (a podcast episode, an interview, a webinar) and turns it into accurate, editable text you can publish, caption, or repurpose. A few tools straddle both, but most are clearly built for one.

Pick by use case first, then by accuracy. A sales team logging calls into a CRM has very different needs from a podcaster captioning episodes for search. This guide groups the best tools by what they are actually for, with rough pricing so you can shortlist fast.

## Live meeting assistants

These tools join your calls, transcribe in real time, and produce notes, summaries, and action items afterward. The transcript is often secondary to what the tool does with it. The value is in not having to take notes while also paying attention.

### Otter.ai

Otter is the default meeting assistant for a lot of teams, and the one most people try first. It joins Zoom, Google Meet, and Microsoft Teams calls, transcribes live, and produces an automated summary with action items when the meeting ends. Its assistant can also answer questions about what was said and pull out decisions across a history of meetings.

The live transcript is searchable and shareable, which makes it useful well beyond the meeting itself. For solo users and small teams, it covers most of what you need without much setup.

**Best for:** Individuals and teams that want reliable live meeting notes with searchable transcripts and summaries.

**Pricing:** Free tier with monthly transcription limits; Pro from around $17/month; Business and Enterprise tiers for teams.

### Fireflies.ai

Fireflies leans harder into team workflows and integrations. It records and transcribes calls across the major platforms, then routes the output where your team already works: CRMs like Salesforce and HubSpot, Slack, project tools, and a searchable internal knowledge base of every call. Its conversation intelligence features tag topics, sentiment, and talk-time, which matters for sales and customer teams.

If your problem is not just capturing notes but getting them into systems automatically, Fireflies is usually the stronger pick over a simpler recorder.

**Best for:** Sales, customer success, and ops teams that need call notes flowing into a CRM and shared stack.

**Pricing:** Free tier; paid plans from around $18 per seat per month.

### Fathom

Fathom built its reputation on an unusually generous free plan. It records, transcribes, and summarizes meetings, generates clips of key moments, and syncs to your CRM, with much of that available at no cost for individuals. The summaries are fast and clean, and you can ask it to highlight a moment during the call to find it later.

For a single user or a small team testing whether a meeting assistant is worth it, Fathom is the easiest no-risk entry point in this list.

**Best for:** Individuals and small teams that want a capable meeting recorder without paying upfront.

**Pricing:** Free for core recording and summaries; Team and Premium tiers from around $19/month.

### tl;dv

tl;dv (short for "too long; didn't view") is a recorder built around building a library of calls you can search, clip, and learn from. It transcribes meetings, timestamps highlights, and is popular with sales teams for coaching, since you can pull moments from real calls to show what worked. It supports a wide range of languages and integrates with CRMs and collaboration tools.

It overlaps with Fireflies and Grain, and the choice between them often comes down to which integrations and clip workflows fit your team.

**Best for:** Sales teams that want a searchable call library with highlights for coaching and review.

**Pricing:** Free tier; paid plans from around $18 per seat per month.

### Grain

Grain is clip-first. Its core idea is that the most valuable part of a recorded call is usually a 30-second moment, and it makes capturing and sharing those moments fast. It records and transcribes meetings, then lets you turn highlights into shareable clips that drop into Slack, a CRM note, or a deal record.

For revenue teams that pass customer quotes and call moments around constantly, that focus pays off. As a general note-taker it is fine, but the clip workflow is the reason to choose it.

**Best for:** Revenue and customer teams that share specific call moments rather than full transcripts.

**Pricing:** Free tier; paid plans from around $19 per seat per month.

## File-based and high-accuracy transcription

These services take a finished recording and turn it into accurate, editable text. Accuracy, language support, and editing tools matter more here than live features, because the output is something you publish, caption, or quote rather than skim once.

### Rev

Rev is the name to know when accuracy is the priority. It offers both AI transcription at a low per-minute rate and human transcription with a guaranteed accuracy level for audio that is noisy, technical, or legally sensitive. That dual model is the differentiator: you can run most files through automated transcription and escalate the few that need to be near-perfect to human transcribers.

It also handles captions and subtitles, which makes it a one-stop option for teams that need both transcripts and accessible video.

**Best for:** Anyone who needs the option of guaranteed high accuracy, including legal, medical, and research use.

**Pricing:** AI transcription from around $0.25 per minute; human transcription from around $1.99 per minute.

### Sonix

Sonix is built for publishers and content teams that process a steady stream of files. It transcribes quickly across many languages, then drops you into a polished editor where the text is synced to the audio, so correcting a word jumps you to the exact moment it was spoken. It also does translation, subtitles, and automated analysis.

The editing experience is the reason to pick it. If your work involves cleaning up transcripts before they ship, Sonix removes most of the friction.

**Best for:** Publishers and content teams that transcribe regularly and need a strong editing workflow.

**Pricing:** Pay-as-you-go from around $5 per hour; standard and premium subscriptions for higher volume.

### Trint

Trint is aimed at newsrooms and large organizations where multiple people work on the same transcripts. It transcribes in many languages and adds collaborative editing, so reporters and producers can verify, annotate, and pull quotes from the same document. Enterprise features like permissions, workspaces, and security are part of the pitch.

For a solo creator it is more tool than you need. For a team that treats transcripts as a shared working document, the collaboration and control are worth the higher price.

**Best for:** Newsrooms and large teams that collaborate on and verify transcripts together.

**Pricing:** From around $80 per seat per month, with enterprise tiers for larger organizations.

### Happy Scribe

Happy Scribe covers transcription and subtitling across a very wide set of languages, which makes it a go-to for anyone working in or across multiple languages. It offers both automated and human-made transcripts and captions, an in-browser editor, and export to the subtitle formats video platforms expect. The pay-as-you-go option suits irregular workloads.

If your main job is captions, or your content spans languages, its breadth is the selling point.

**Best for:** Subtitling and multilingual transcription where language coverage is the priority.

**Pricing:** Subscriptions from around $17/month; pay-as-you-go per hour also available.

### Notta

Notta is the budget all-rounder. It handles both real-time transcription and uploaded files, supports a large number of languages, and offers AI summaries, all at a starting price well below the premium services. It integrates with the main meeting platforms and exports to common formats.

The accuracy is solid for clear audio, though for difficult recordings the higher-accuracy services still pull ahead. For most everyday transcription on a tight budget, Notta covers a lot of ground cheaply.

**Best for:** Budget-conscious users who want both live and file transcription with broad language support.

**Pricing:** Free tier; paid plans from around $9/month.

## Editing-integrated and developer tools

Two entries do not fit the meeting-versus-file split. One uses transcription as the foundation of an editing tool, the other is the model that powers much of this category.

### Descript

Descript transcribes your audio or video and then lets you edit the media by editing the text. Delete a sentence from the transcript and it cuts the corresponding audio and video; its features remove filler words, fix mistakes by typing, and even regenerate misspoken words with a voice clone. It is less a transcription service than an editor that happens to run on an accurate transcript.

For podcasters and video creators, that model changes how editing feels. The transcription is the means, not the product, but its quality is what makes the editing usable.

**Best for:** Podcasters and video creators who want to edit audio and video by editing text.

**Pricing:** Free tier; paid plans from around $24 per month.

### OpenAI Whisper

Whisper is OpenAI's open-source speech-to-text model, and it is the engine behind a large share of the tools in this guide, directly or indirectly. For developers, it is available both as open-source code you can self-host and as a hosted API, which makes it the practical choice for building transcription into your own product or pipeline.

You do not get an interface, summaries, or an editor; you get accurate, multilingual transcription you can call programmatically and cheaply at scale. If you are a builder rather than an end user, this is where most custom transcription starts.

**Best for:** Developers building transcription into their own applications or batch pipelines.

**Pricing:** Open-source and free to self-host; hosted API from around $0.006 per minute.

## How to choose

Picking a transcription tool comes down to a few honest questions about what you are transcribing and why.

**Live or file?** If you need notes from meetings as they happen, you want a meeting assistant: Otter, Fireflies, Fathom, tl;dv, or Grain. If you have finished recordings to turn into publishable text, you want a file-based service: Rev, Sonix, Trint, Happy Scribe, or Notta. Trying to force one type to do the other job is where teams waste money.

**Accuracy or price?** For clear audio in a quiet room, the cheap automated tools are good enough and the difference is marginal. For noisy, accented, technical, or legally sensitive audio, accuracy is worth paying for, and a service with a human option like Rev earns its cost. Match the spend to the consequence of an error.

**How many languages?** If you work across languages or need subtitles in several, prioritize breadth: Happy Scribe and Notta cover a wide set, and Whisper handles many languages out of the box. A tool that nails English but stumbles elsewhere will not serve a multilingual workflow.

**Where does the output need to go?** A transcript that lives in a tab nobody opens is wasted. If the value is getting notes into a CRM or shared stack, integrations matter more than raw transcript quality, which is where Fireflies and the meeting assistants pull ahead. If the value is published content, the editing and export tools of Sonix or Descript matter more.

Most teams end up with one meeting assistant for live calls and, if they publish content, one file-based or editing tool for recordings. Start with the job that comes up most, prove it works, and add the second tool only when you have a real need for it. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best AI transcription tool overall in 2026?

For most people, Otter.ai is the best all-around choice because it handles live meeting transcription, summaries, and searchable notes with very little setup, and it integrates with the major calling platforms. If your main need is finished recordings rather than live calls, Rev or Sonix are stronger because they prioritize accuracy and editing. The best tool depends on whether your job is live meetings or file-based transcription.

### What is the best free AI transcription tool?

Fathom has one of the most generous free plans for meeting recording, summaries, and clips, which makes it the easiest no-cost starting point for calls. Otter, Fireflies, and Notta also offer free tiers with monthly limits. For developers, OpenAI Whisper is free and open-source to self-host, giving you accurate transcription at no licensing cost if you can run it yourself.

### What is the most accurate AI transcription tool?

Rev is the most reliable choice when accuracy is critical because it offers human transcription with a guaranteed accuracy level alongside its automated option, which matters for noisy, accented, technical, or legally sensitive audio. Among fully automated tools, Sonix and the OpenAI Whisper model both perform well on clear recordings. The honest answer is that no automated tool matches human transcription on difficult audio, so escalate the hardest files to a human service.

### What is the best AI transcription tool for meetings?

For meetings, the best tool depends on what happens after the call. Otter.ai is the strongest general meeting assistant for live notes and summaries, while Fireflies.ai is better if you need notes pushed automatically into a CRM and shared stack. Fathom, tl;dv, and Grain are excellent for recording, clipping, and building a searchable call library, especially for sales teams.

### What is the best AI transcription tool for podcasts and video?

Descript is the standout for podcasts and video because it lets you edit the audio and video by editing the transcript, removing filler words and mistakes by typing rather than scrubbing a timeline. For accurate transcripts and subtitles to publish alongside episodes, Sonix and Happy Scribe are strong, with Happy Scribe especially good for captions across many languages. Many creators use Descript for editing and a dedicated service for final captions.
