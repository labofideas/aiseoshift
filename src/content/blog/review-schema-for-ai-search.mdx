When someone asks ChatGPT or Perplexity "what is the best-rated Italian restaurant near downtown?", the answer leans on ratings that live somewhere. Usually that somewhere is a review platform, a Google Business Profile, or a product page full of real customer reviews.

Review and AggregateRating markup is how a page tells machines "these are reviews, this is the average, this is the count." Done right on the right page, it can earn star snippets in Google. Done on the wrong page, it earns nothing.

The wrong page is more common than you would think. Google does not show review stars for a business's reviews of itself on its own website. Not in the markup. Not through an embedded Google reviews widget either. Product pages, recipes, books, apps and sites that review *other* businesses are a different story.

And a straight answer on AI search: no AI engine has published that it reads AggregateRating markup as a ranking or trust input. Treat any claim that it "boosts AI citations" as a guess. What does help is having real, visible reviews and an accurate picture of your business across the web.

This guide covers the properties of [Schema.org's AggregateRating type](https://schema.org/AggregateRating) and [Review type](https://schema.org/Review), which pages Google allows to use them for star snippets, the self-serving rule, and what review markup can realistically do for AI visibility. If you are new to schema markup, start with [schema markup for AI visibility](/blog/schema-markup-for-ai-visibility/).

## AggregateRating schema, the four core properties

[Schema.org's AggregateRating type](https://schema.org/AggregateRating) is a subtype of Rating, designed to represent the collective assessment of an item based on multiple individual ratings or reviews. It either nests inside the item being rated, such as a Product, or names that item with the `itemReviewed` property. Understanding its four key properties is essential before touching any implementation.

**ratingValue** is the computed average rating, expressed as a number or a string. For a 5-star system where a product has an average of 4.3, this would be `"4.3"`. The value must fall between `worstRating` and `bestRating`. A common error is marking a 4.3-out-of-5 rating as `ratingValue: "4.3"` without specifying `bestRating: "5"`, which leaves the scale ambiguous for parsers and means the rating could be interpreted as 4.3 out of 10.

**reviewCount** is the total number of reviews that contributed to the aggregate. This is the count of text reviews rather than just numeric ratings. The companion property `ratingCount` covers all ratings, including those without written reviews. Google requires at least one of the two. Readers care about volume too: 4.8 from three reviews tells them far less than 4.8 from 847.

**bestRating** is the highest possible rating value in the scale. For most star-rating systems this is `"5"`. For a 10-point scale it would be `"10"`. If omitted, Schema.org assumes a default of 5, but explicit declaration is strongly recommended to avoid ambiguity.

**worstRating** is the lowest possible rating value. For a 5-star scale this is typically `"1"`. The default assumption when omitted is 1, but explicit declaration removes any parsing ambiguity.

A minimal but complete AggregateRating block looks like this:

```json
"aggregateRating": {
  "@type": "AggregateRating",
  "ratingValue": "4.6",
  "reviewCount": "312",
  "bestRating": "5",
  "worstRating": "1"
}
```

## Individual Review schema, properties and purpose

While AggregateRating provides the summary, the [Schema.org Review type](https://schema.org/Review) represents individual review instances. Both can appear on the same page, and both serve distinct citation purposes. AggregateRating answers "how good is this overall?" Review schema answers "what specifically did a real person say about this, and when?"

The key properties for an individual Review are:

**author** should use a `Person` type with at minimum a `name` property. Google requires a valid name here, and recommends only accepting ratings that come with a review comment and the author's name. If the reviewer has a public profile URL, include it in the `author` object's `url` property.

**datePublished** should use ISO 8601 date format: `"2026-01-14"`. Google lists it as recommended. For readers, recent reviews say far more about a product today than a wall of reviews from 2019.

**reviewBody** is the full text of the review. It is the part a person, or a system summarizing the page, can actually quote. Keep the marked-up text the same as what is shown on the page.

**reviewRating** nests a Rating object that records the specific rating for this individual review:

```json
"reviewRating": {
  "@type": "Rating",
  "ratingValue": "5",
  "bestRating": "5",
  "worstRating": "1"
}
```

A well-formed individual Review block within a Product schema looks like this:

```json
{
  "@type": "Review",
  "author": {
    "@type": "Person",
    "name": "Sarah M.",
    "url": "https://example.com/users/sarah-m"
  },
  "datePublished": "2026-02-28",
  "reviewBody": "I have been using this project management tool for six months across a team of twelve people. The onboarding was seamless and the task dependency visualization is genuinely the best I have seen at this price point. The mobile app needs work, but the desktop experience is excellent.",
  "reviewRating": {
    "@type": "Rating",
    "ratingValue": "4",
    "bestRating": "5",
    "worstRating": "1"
  }
}
```

## Which pages Google allows to show review stars

Schema.org lets you attach Review and AggregateRating to a long list of types. Google is much stricter about which ones can earn a review snippet. Its [review snippet documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet) supports ratings for:

- **Product, Book, Recipe, Movie, Event, Course, Software App**, plus a handful of media types like Episode, Game and MusicRecording.
- **LocalBusiness and Organization, but only on sites that capture reviews about *other* businesses or organizations.** A restaurant guide reviewing restaurants qualifies. A restaurant marking up its own rating does not.

That second point is where most local businesses go wrong, so it gets its own section below.

Two more rules from the same page catch people out. The reviews must be visible on the marked-up page: if you mark up an aggregate rating, users must be able to see that rating. And the review must be about one specific item, not a category or a list of items.

The `@type` of the parent entity should match what is actually being reviewed. Using `Product` to mark up a restaurant, or `LocalBusiness` to mark up a software app, is a mismatch that validators flag.

## The self-serving rule: why your business can't give itself stars

Google's rule, in its own words: if the entity being reviewed controls the reviews about itself, its pages that use `LocalBusiness` or any other type of `Organization` structured data are ineligible for the star review feature.

Google's example makes it concrete. A review about entity A placed on entity A's own website is self-serving, **whether it sits directly in the structured data or comes through an embedded third-party widget**, such as a Google Business reviews widget or a Facebook reviews widget.

So all of these get no stars:

- A restaurant adding AggregateRating to its own Restaurant markup.
- A law firm marking up client testimonials on its own site.
- A dentist embedding a Google reviews widget and wrapping it in AggregateRating.
- An agency adding a "4.9 from 200 clients" rating to its homepage Organization schema.

Even if every review is real.

Your markup does no harm here, and it can still describe the page accurately. It simply will not produce a star snippet. For a local business, the stars people see in Google come from your [Google Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/), written by customers. That is where the effort belongs: ask happy customers for reviews and reply to the ones you get.

Your **LocalBusiness** markup is still worth doing without the rating. Name, address, phone, opening hours and cuisine help search engines understand who you are:

```json
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Restaurant",
  "name": "Trattoria Napoli",
  "url": "https://trattorianapoli.com",
  "telephone": "+1-312-555-0182",
  "address": {
    "@type": "PostalAddress",
    "streetAddress": "847 N Wells Street",
    "addressLocality": "Chicago",
    "addressRegion": "IL",
    "postalCode": "60610",
    "addressCountry": "US"
  },
  "servesCuisine": "Italian",
  "openingHoursSpecification": [
    {
      "@type": "OpeningHoursSpecification",
      "dayOfWeek": ["Monday", "Tuesday", "Wednesday", "Thursday"],
      "opens": "17:00",
      "closes": "22:00"
    },
    {
      "@type": "OpeningHoursSpecification",
      "dayOfWeek": ["Friday", "Saturday"],
      "opens": "17:00",
      "closes": "23:00"
    }
  ]
}
```

Keep those details identical to your Business Profile. Mismatched hours or addresses confuse people and machines alike.

## Where review markup does earn stars

The rule flips when the reviews are about something you sell or something you review, not about you as a business.

**Product pages** are the big one. An online shop with real customer reviews on each product page can mark up those reviews and their average, nested in the Product markup. A software company can do the same on a SoftwareApplication page.

**Review and listing sites** that capture reviews about other businesses can mark up LocalBusiness ratings, because the business being reviewed does not control them.

**Recipes, books, courses and events** follow the product logic: real ratings from real users of that specific item.

The aggregate must reflect the reviews on your own site. Google's guidelines say plainly: **don't aggregate reviews or ratings from other websites.** A shop cannot pull its Trustpilot average into product markup.

## Google's review markup policies, the short list

From Google's [review snippet guidelines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet):

**No fake or undisclosed incentivized reviews.** That covers reviews not based on a genuine experience, and reviews written in exchange for money, discounts, vouchers or free products without clearly and prominently disclosing it.

**Reviews must be visible.** If you mark up reviews, users must be able to read the text and rating on that page. If you mark up an aggregate, users must see the aggregate.

**Ratings for local businesses must come straight from users.** No human editors creating, curating or compiling them.

**No aggregating from other sites.** Covered above, and the most common product-page mistake after self-serving reviews.

The penalty is specific. If Google issues a structured data manual action, it ignores the structured data on the page, though the page can still appear in Search. And even fully compliant markup comes with no promise: Google does not guarantee rich results.

## What review markup can do for AI search

Here is the honest version, because a lot of advice on this topic is guesswork dressed as fact.

No AI engine, not Google's AI features, not ChatGPT, not Perplexity, has published that it reads AggregateRating markup as a trust score or a citation signal. Nobody outside those companies knows the weighting. Be wary of anyone who claims to.

What we do know:

Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode draw on pages from Google's index, and Google advises keeping important information in text on the page. If your reviews are real and readable on the page, they are part of what a system can read and quote.

When people ask an assistant for the "best-rated" option, the ratings it can find tend to live on review platforms and business profiles. A strong Google Business Profile and genuine reviews on the platforms your customers use matter more for local recommendations than markup on your own site.

Consistency helps everywhere. The same name, address, hours and offering across your site, your Business Profile and the directories you appear in makes your business easier to identify. This is the "corroboration across sources" idea the [AI SEO audit checklist](/blog/ai-seo-audit-checklist/) checks for.

So use review markup where it is allowed and accurate, mainly product-type pages. Do not expect it to move AI citations on its own. The [AI SEO Shift framework](/ai-seo-shift/) covers how entity clarity, visible content and third-party presence fit together, and [how to get cited by AI search systems](/blog/how-to-get-cited-by-ai-search-systems/) goes deeper on the citation side.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does AggregateRating schema get star ratings in Google search results?**

It can, on eligible pages. AggregateRating is how you describe an average rating for Google's review snippet. The item must be an eligible type, such as a product, recipe or book, the reviews must be visible on the page, and they must not be self-serving. Even then, Google does not guarantee the stars will show.

**Can a local business show review stars from its own website?**

No. Google treats reviews about a business placed on that business's own website as self-serving, and pages using LocalBusiness or Organization markup are ineligible for the star review feature. That includes reviews pulled in through an embedded Google Business or Facebook reviews widget. A local business's stars in Google come from its Google Business Profile.

**Can I add AggregateRating to a product page that loads reviews through a third-party widget?**

Only if the reviews were collected on your own site and are visible to users on that page, and the widget output is something crawlers can see. Google's guidelines say not to aggregate reviews or ratings from other websites, so an average imported from an outside platform does not qualify. If the widget only loads after the page renders in the browser, check with the Rich Results Test that Google can see the reviews.

**How many individual Review entries should I include on a product page?**

Google sets no number. If you include several individual reviews, also include an aggregate rating of them. Mark up reviews that are actually shown on the page, with a real author name and rating. A believable mix of ratings serves readers better than only five-star picks.

**Should law firms and consultancies use Review schema?**

Not for star snippets about themselves. A firm's own client reviews on its own site are self-serving under Google's rules, so Organization or LocalBusiness rating markup there is ineligible for stars. The better investment is genuine reviews on the firm's Google Business Profile and on the platforms clients in that field actually use.

**Does review schema help ChatGPT or Perplexity recommend my business?**

No AI company has said it uses review markup as a ranking or trust signal, so nobody can promise that. What reliably helps is having real reviews where these tools look, such as review platforms and your Business Profile, and making the facts about your business clear in plain text on your own site.
