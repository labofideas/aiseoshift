A marketing plan template needs eight sections to actually get used: situation analysis, goals, audience, positioning, channel mix, budget, a calendar and KPIs. Skip any one of them and what you have is a list of good intentions with a due date, not a plan. Below is the full structure in copyable markdown tables, followed by a complete hypothetical example so you can see what real inputs look like instead of placeholder text.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - A marketing plan needs 8 sections to function: situation, goals, audience, positioning, channels, budget, calendar, KPIs.
> - Goals and KPIs are the two sections people skip, and they're the two that make the rest of the document checkable.
> - Budget by channel, not a lump sum, forces the tradeoff decisions a plan is supposed to make.
> - A 90-day calendar beats a 12-month one for anything that isn't a large, slow-moving org.
> - The template below is built to be copied into a doc and filled in the same afternoon, not workshopped for a month.

## Why most marketing plan templates fail before they get used

Most templates fail for one of two reasons. Either they're too long, so nobody opens the doc after week one, or they're missing the sections that create accountability, so there's no goal to measure against and no budget line to defend in the next planning meeting.

A marketing plan is not a brand document. It's an operating document. If a new hire can't read it and know what to do Monday morning, it hasn't done its job.

## A filled-in example: a hypothetical coffee subscription brand

Say you run Maple & Co., a hypothetical six-person DTC coffee subscription brand doing about $600,000 a year, mostly through Meta ads and an email list of 18,000 subscribers.

**Situation.** Growth stage. Two competitors undercut on price; Maple & Co. wins on sourcing story and a stronger unboxing experience. Biggest constraint: rising Meta CPMs are squeezing new customer acquisition.

**Goals.** Grow monthly recurring subscribers from 1,400 to 1,700 in 90 days. Secondary goal: cut 90-day churn from 22% to 18%.

**Audience.** Primary: home coffee drinkers, 28-45, who already buy specialty beans but want convenience. Secondary: gift buyers, mostly in November and December.

**Positioning.** "For home coffee drinkers who are tired of bitter grocery-store bags, Maple & Co. is the subscription that ships beans roasted within 72 hours, unlike bigger subscription boxes, because we roast in batches of under 50 lbs and ship the same week."

**Channels and budget**, on a hypothetical $9,000 monthly marketing budget:

| Channel | Monthly budget | % of total | Primary goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paid social | $4,000 | 44% | New subscriber acquisition |
| Email | $500 (tool cost) | 6% | Retention, reduce churn |
| Content / SEO | $1,500 | 17% | Lower long-term CAC |
| Influencer seeding | $2,000 | 22% | Trust, UGC for ads |
| Packaging / referral incentive | $1,000 | 11% | Word of mouth |
| **Total** | **$9,000** | **100%** | |

**90-day calendar**, abbreviated: weeks 1-2, launch a win-back flow for lapsed subscribers; weeks 3-4, start seeding 10 micro-influencers; weeks 5-8, test two new Meta creative angles against the control; weeks 9-12, run a referral push timed to the holiday gifting window.

**KPIs.** Monthly recurring subscribers (target 1,700), CAC (target under $38), 90-day churn (target under 18%), email-attributed revenue (target 15% of total).

Nothing in that example needs to be clever. It needs to be specific enough that someone could check it in 90 days and say, clearly, whether it worked.

## Common mistakes when filling this in

**Writing goals as activities instead of outcomes.** "Post more on Instagram" is an activity. "Grow Instagram-attributed signups from 40 to 90 a month" is a goal. If a line in your goals table could be done badly and still technically count as complete, rewrite it.

**Skipping the budget split and writing one lump number.** "$9,000 a month on marketing" tells you nothing about whether you're overspending on a channel that isn't working. Splitting it by channel forces the argument about tradeoffs to happen on paper, in October, instead of out loud, in a panic, in December.

**Copying a competitor's channel mix without checking the audience fit.** A competitor's heavy TikTok spend only matters if your audience table says your buyers are actually there. Fill in section 3 honestly before section 5.

**Setting a 12-month calendar in granular detail.** Detailed planning past 90 days is almost always wrong by month four, because a channel changes, a competitor moves, or a campaign underperforms. Plan the next 90 days in detail and the rest in themes only.

**Leaving the KPI table's "how it's tracked" column blank.** A target with no tracking method attached doesn't get checked. If you can't say today where the number comes from, the number won't exist in 90 days either.

## Adapting the template by business type

The eight sections stay the same. What changes is which ones carry the most weight.

**B2B SaaS.** The audience section usually needs two rows, not one: the economic buyer and the day-to-day user, since they care about different things. Budget skews toward content, SEO and sales enablement over paid social. KPIs should include a pipeline or demo-booked metric, not just traffic.

**Local service business** (contractors, clinics, salons). The situation analysis should include your Google Business Profile standing and review count, since local buyers check that before anything else. Channel budget usually concentrates in local search and referral/review generation rather than being spread across five channels. The 90-day calendar should map to local seasonality (storm season, tax season, wedding season) rather than generic launch windows.

**Ecommerce / DTC**, like the Maple & Co. example above. Budget and calendar both need to flex around a small number of high-stakes dates: Black Friday-Cyber Monday, a seasonal gifting window, a product drop. KPIs should separate new-customer CAC from returning-customer metrics, since blending them hides whether paid acquisition is actually profitable.

## How to use this without it turning into shelfware

The plan fails the moment nobody rereads it. Three habits fix that:

- **Put the KPI table somewhere visible**, not buried on page three. A shared dashboard or even a pinned Slack message works better than a doc nobody reopens.
- **Review the calendar weekly, the KPIs monthly, the whole plan quarterly.** Different sections decay at different speeds.
- **Assign an owner to every row**, not just every section. "Marketing team" is not an owner. A name is.

## The bottom line

A marketing plan template is only as good as the two sections people skip: goals with real numbers, and KPIs with real targets. Everything else, the situation analysis, the positioning line, the channel table, exists to support those two. Fill in the template above with your own numbers, not placeholder text, and you'll have something worth reopening in 90 days instead of something you wrote once and forgot.
