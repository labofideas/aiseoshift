Let me describe what Google search looks like in May 2026.

You type a question. Before you finish typing, Google has already read a dozen websites, synthesized the answer, and displayed it at the top of the page in a polished AI summary. Below that summary, smaller than before, are the links to the websites Google read to generate that summary. Most people never scroll down to them.

Google calls this progress. They call it [AI Mode](/ai-mode-seo/). They call it the biggest upgrade to their search box in 25 years. Elizabeth Reid, Google's head of Search, stood on stage at I/O 2026 and said it plainly: "Google search is AI search through and through."

She is right. Search has become AI search.

The question nobody at that keynote addressed is what happens to the websites that created the information Google's AI just took.

## The numbers tell the story before anyone admits it

Sixty percent of Google searches now end without a click. When AI Overviews appear, that number jumps to 83 percent.

Sit with that for a minute. Eighty-three percent.

HubSpot estimates it lost 70 to 80 percent of its organic traffic. Chegg, the education company whose entire business depends on people visiting its website to get answers, saw a 49 percent decline. DMG Media documented drops as steep as 89 percent for some queries. NPR called it an "extinction-level event" for online news publishers.

And these are the companies with resources to measure what happened. Thousands of smaller publishers, independent bloggers, and niche content creators are watching their traffic evaporate without the analytics teams to quantify it.

Google's response, paraphrased: "AI Overviews actually increase engagement with search." Which is true. People do engage more with Google search. They just engage less with your website.

## Here is where it gets uncomfortable

The content Google uses to generate its AI answers does not appear out of thin air. It comes from the websites that spent years creating it.

Your blog post explaining how to fix a leaking faucet. A nutritionist's guide to managing cholesterol. A lawyer's breakdown of tenant rights. A small business owner's comparison of accounting software. All of that expertise, all of that effort, all of those hours of writing and editing and fact-checking, gets ingested by Google's AI and served back to the user as a neat paragraph at the top of the results page.

The user gets the answer. Google gets the engagement metrics. The website gets a citation link that 83 percent of users never click.

This is the deal Google is offering in 2026. Your content powers their AI. In exchange, you get a footnote.

## The funnel that built the internet is broken

Here is how content marketing worked for twenty years. You create genuinely useful informational content. That content attracts visitors through search. Some of those visitors explore your site, trust your expertise, and eventually buy your product or hire your service. The content was the top of the funnel. The traffic was the mechanism. The conversion happened because people landed on your site and experienced your brand firsthand.

Google AI search breaks this at the traffic stage. The informational content still exists. It still gets indexed. It still gets cited in AI summaries. But the visitor never arrives at your website because they already got what they needed from Google's summary.

The top of the funnel has been amputated. The informational query that used to bring people into your world now keeps them inside Google's world.

And the businesses that depended on that funnel are being pushed toward one remaining option.

## The ad machine gets hungrier

Google Ads CPC rose 12 percent year over year in Q1 2026. The steepest annual increase since 2021. Industry projections suggest another 8 to 10 percent through Q4.

This is not a coincidence. When organic traffic declines, businesses have to pay to maintain visibility. More businesses competing for the same ad inventory means higher prices. Google simultaneously reduces the organic opportunity and profits from the desperation that follows.

I have watched this cycle play out with clients. Their informational content used to generate thousands of visits per month. Those visits led to email signups, demo requests, and sales calls. The content justified itself through the pipeline it created.

Now the same content generates a fraction of the traffic. The pipeline shrinks. The marketing team panics. The CFO approves more ad spend. CPC goes up. The cost of acquiring a customer climbs. Margins compress.

And Google's ad revenue grows. It crossed $200 billion in 2025 and is accelerating.

I do not think Google planned this as a deliberate squeeze. I think they built the AI features to compete with ChatGPT and Perplexity, and the economic consequences for publishers are a byproduct they are comfortable with. That distinction does not matter much to the businesses watching their traffic disappear.

## The "personalized assistant" is the quiet part said loud

At I/O 2026, Google announced that AI search now integrates personal context. Your emails. Your calendar. Your location history. Your purchase patterns. Search is no longer a tool you use to find things. It is an assistant that anticipates what you need before you ask.

Google described this as making search more helpful. And for the user, it is. If Google already knows you have a dinner reservation at 7 and a meeting across town at 5, it can proactively suggest you leave by 4:30 and recommend a coffee shop near your meeting location.

Convenient? Absolutely. But follow the logic.

If Google already knows what you need, and Google's AI can generate the answer without sending you to a website, then what is the purpose of the website in this system?

The website becomes a training input. Something Google reads so its AI can be helpful. Not something the user visits.

The assistant metaphor is revealing. An assistant reads documents, summarizes them, and presents the summary to you. You never read the original documents yourself. That is exactly what Google AI search does with web content.

## The irony nobody talks about

Google's AI can only be helpful because the open web exists. Every AI summary, every AI Overview, every confident answer that Google serves at the top of the results page was built from content that real people created on real websites.

If those websites stop creating content because the traffic incentive disappears, the AI summaries get worse. Google needs the open web to function. But Google's current trajectory actively undermines the economic model that sustains the open web.

This is not a hypothetical. One-third of publishers are now considering blocking Google's AI from accessing their content. Some already have. If the trend accelerates, Google will face a content quality problem that no amount of compute can solve.

You cannot summarize content that no longer exists.

## So what do you actually do?

I would love to tell you there is a simple fix. There is not. But there are strategic responses that work better than hoping Google changes course.

The first and most important shift is mental. Stop thinking about Google as a traffic source and start thinking about it as one of many [surfaces where your brand appears](/ai-seo-shift/). AI Overviews are a visibility surface. ChatGPT is a visibility surface. Perplexity is a visibility surface. Your email list, your YouTube channel, your LinkedIn presence, your podcast, your community. All surfaces. The goal is not to win traffic from one platform. The goal is to be the brand AI systems trust and cite across all of them.

The second shift is structural. [Content optimized for AI extraction](/blog/how-to-structure-pages-ai-extraction/) gets cited more often and more accurately than content optimized purely for rankings. Clear definitions, structured data, explicit factual statements, and proper [schema markup](/blog/schema-markup-for-ai-visibility/) make your content easier for AI to reference correctly. If Google is going to summarize your content anyway, make sure the summary is accurate and attributes you properly.

The third shift is economic. If informational content no longer drives traffic reliably, the content strategy needs to create value that survives the zero-click filter. Original research that cannot be summarized in a paragraph. Interactive tools that require visiting your site. Community experiences that only exist on your platform. Direct relationships through email and subscriptions that bypass search entirely.

And yes, the fourth shift is acceptance. The traffic numbers from 2023 are not coming back. The model where you write a blog post and watch the visits pour in from Google is over for informational queries. That is painful. But building strategy around nostalgia for how search used to work is worse than building strategy around how search actually works now.

## The uncomfortable truth about ads

I understand why businesses are pouring money into Google Ads. When organic traffic drops, ads feel like the only lever left. And for some businesses, in some markets, ads remain an effective channel.

But here is what I see happening. CPC keeps climbing. The businesses that relied on organic traffic are all shifting to ads simultaneously, driving prices up for everyone. The ROI of Google Ads is declining for most industries, not because the ads do not work, but because the cost has outpaced the return.

You are paying Google to restore the visibility that Google took away.

I am not saying stop running ads. I am saying that doubling your ad budget because your organic traffic declined is solving a symptom, not the problem. The problem is over-dependence on Google as a channel. The solution is [building presence across every surface where your audience finds information](/blog/omnichannel-ai-brand-presence/), including the AI systems that are rapidly becoming the primary way people discover content.

## What Google will not tell you

Google will not tell you that their AI features are designed to keep users inside Google's ecosystem. They will frame everything as "making search more helpful." And from the user's perspective, it is more helpful. The answers are faster, more comprehensive, and more personalized than ever.

But from the publisher's perspective, from the small business owner's perspective, from the independent creator's perspective, "more helpful" means Google extracting more value from your content while returning less of it.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is a business model. Google monetizes attention. The longer users stay on Google, the more ads they see. AI Overviews that answer questions without requiring clicks to external websites keep users on Google longer.

Every percentage point of zero-click increase is a percentage point of attention that Google captures and monetizes.

## Where this leaves us

Search as we knew it is over. That sentence has been written a hundred times and it was premature every time until now. But Google replacing the search box with an AI assistant that reads your emails, checks your calendar, and generates answers from other people's content is not an incremental change.

It is a new system.

And in this new system, the websites that adapt will survive and the websites that keep optimizing for a version of Google that no longer exists will slowly fade.

Adaptation does not mean surrendering to Google. It means building something Google cannot replicate. A brand that people seek out directly. A community that exists independent of any algorithm. Content so original that the AI summary is obviously incomplete without visiting your site. Relationships with your audience that do not require a search engine as intermediary.

Google is taking your content and calling it a feature. You cannot stop them. But you can build something they cannot take.
