## Why E-E-A-T matters more for accounting than for most verticals

Three factors:

1. **YMYL classification.** Google explicitly weights expertise, experience, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness more heavily for content that could affect a user's financial wellbeing. Tax content is the textbook example.
2. **AI engines cross-reference credentials.** ChatGPT and Perplexity check whether claimed CPAs actually hold active licenses. They cross-reference firm names against state board databases. Mismatches fail extraction.
3. **Regulatory and ethical exposure.** Accounting content is subject to state board rules, IRS Circular 230, and AICPA professional standards. Anonymous content can violate these. Credentialed content with proper disclosures complies.

For broader context, see our [E-E-A-T guide](/seo/eeat/).

## Element 1: Named CPA author bylines

Every piece of accounting content needs a named author. Not "Our Team," not "Editorial Staff," not the firm name as author. A specific CPA with verifiable credentials.

The byline should appear:

- At the top of every page, immediately under the title
- Linked to a dedicated author bio page
- With the author's credentials abbreviation (CPA, EA, MST)

Example: "By Jane Smith, CPA · Updated May 27, 2026"

If an article is written by a non-CPA staff member and reviewed by a CPA, surface both: "By Sarah Johnson · Reviewed by Jane Smith, CPA"

## Element 2: Full author bio pages

Every CPA byline links to a dedicated bio page. The bio page is not a paragraph — it is a substantive 400 to 800 word page that signals expertise.

The required elements:

- High-quality professional photo
- Full name and credentials
- License state(s) and active license number
- Years of experience
- Specialty areas (with depth — not "tax" but "real estate tax, cost segregation, 1031 exchanges")
- Education
- Professional memberships (AICPA, state CPA society)
- Publications, speaking engagements, media mentions
- Links to representative content authored by this CPA

The bio page is also the canonical location for `Person` schema with `hasCredential` markup. See the companion post on [accounting firm schema markup](/blog/accounting-firm-schema-markup-services-author/).

## Element 3: Credential verification surfaces

AI engines cross-reference claimed credentials against public databases. Make this verification easy:

- License number visible on the bio page
- Link to the state board's license lookup
- AICPA membership disclosed if applicable
- State CPA society membership disclosed

If a CPA holds multiple state licenses, list all of them. If the CPA has additional certifications (EA, CFP, CITP), surface them. More verifiable credentials make extraction easier.

## Element 4: Topical authority maps

E-E-A-T is partly about claimed expertise and partly about demonstrated topical authority. A CPA claiming real estate tax expertise should have 15 to 30 published pieces on real estate tax topics under their byline.

Build topical authority deliberately:

- Each CPA picks 2 to 4 specialty areas
- Each specialty area gets a content map of 15 to 30 sub-topics
- Each sub-topic gets a published piece under the CPA's byline
- The CPA's bio page links to the content cluster

When AI engines query "real estate tax CPA," they extract the named CPAs with demonstrated depth on the topic.

## Element 5: Currency and review cadence

Tax law changes constantly. Content with a 2022 publication date and no review since reads as stale. The fix:

- **Publication date** on every article
- **Updated date** when content is reviewed and revised
- **Review schedule** maintained internally — at minimum, annual review for evergreen content, quarterly review for time-sensitive content
- **Visible "Last reviewed by [CPA name] on [date]"** notation on pages

AI engines weigh recency heavily for YMYL content. A piece reviewed in the last 12 months by a credentialed CPA carries dramatically more weight than a piece that has not been touched in three years.

## Element 6: Citations and references

Substantive accounting content cites authoritative sources:

- IRS publications and revenue rulings
- Tax code sections
- Treasury regulations
- Court decisions
- AICPA pronouncements
- State board rulings

Inline citations with links to primary sources signal scholarly rigor and let AI engines verify claims. Content with no citations is harder to verify and gets filtered more aggressively.

For broader context on building authority signals, see our post on [SEO authority, not rankings](/blog/seo-authority-not-rankings/).

## Element 7: Disclosures and limitations

YMYL content requires disclosures. Common disclosures for accounting content:

- "This article is for general information and does not constitute tax or legal advice."
- "Consult a qualified CPA for advice specific to your situation."
- "Tax law changes frequently. Information current as of [date]."

Required disclosures vary by state. Check with your state board.

Proper disclosures signal trustworthiness and reduce regulatory risk. AI engines extract disclosure language as a positive trust signal.

## Building the infrastructure: a 30-day sprint

Realistic 30-day E-E-A-T build for a firm starting from generic content:

- **Week 1:** Audit existing content for byline status. Catalog which CPAs will be primary authors and their specialty areas.
- **Week 2:** Build bio pages for each named CPA author. Photos, credentials, schema markup.
- **Week 3:** Backfill bylines on existing content. Surface "Last reviewed by" notation on top 20 pages.
- **Week 4:** Citation audit on top 20 pages. Add primary source links where missing.

Twelve weeks in, the firm has a complete E-E-A-T infrastructure ready for ongoing content production.

## The compounding effect

E-E-A-T signals compound. A firm with three years of CPA-authored content under named bylines outranks a firm with five years of anonymous content. The gap widens as AI engines weigh historical attribution more heavily.

A firm that publishes weekly CPA-authored content for 24 months has roughly 100 bylined pieces across 3 to 5 specialty areas. That depth is structurally hard to replicate from behind.

## FAQs

### What if I do not have any CPAs on staff?

Then you should not be publishing accounting content as a firm. Tax preparers, EAs, and bookkeepers can author content, but the byline must accurately reflect their credentials. Misrepresenting credentials is both an SEO problem and a legal one.

### Can I use freelance CPA authors?

Yes, with full byline attribution. The CPA's bio page should disclose the contractor relationship and the CPA must be willing to be publicly associated with the firm.

### How do I handle content authored by non-CPA staff?

Two acceptable patterns: (1) byline the non-CPA author and add "Reviewed by [CPA name]" notation, or (2) for highly technical content, byline the CPA who reviewed and approved the content even if a non-CPA drafted it.

### What about ghostwriting?

The named author must have substantive involvement in the content — direction, review, fact-checking, and final approval. A CPA can ghostwrite or supervise ghostwriting, but they need to actually engage with the content.

### Should partners be the primary bylined authors?

Senior CPAs (partners, principals) make the strongest bylines because their credentials are most verifiable and their experience is deepest. But every credentialed CPA on staff can and should byline content in their specialty area.
